Dien Bien Phu and the end of French rule

Why was the United States paying for France’s war in Vietnam?

Truman and Eisenhower decided Vietnam mattered in the Cold War. Every president after them inherited that decision.

  • The story
  • Sources
  • For the test

The short version

  • Eisenhower warned that if Indochina fell to communism, its neighbors would fall next, like a row of dominoes. This Domino Theory guided U.S. policy for years.

  • By 1954 the United States was paying about 80% of France’s war costs. It still had no say in how France fought.

  • France’s defeat at Dien Bien Phu in May 1954 ended its rule in Indochina. That summer, the Geneva Conference split Vietnam at the 17th parallel.

What happened

The story in five short parts. A few end with a question to check yourself.

1945–1946

Ho Chi Minh declares independence, and asks America for help

On September 2, 1945, Ho Chi Minh proclaimed an independent Vietnam in Hanoi, quoting the American Declaration of Independence. France wanted its colony back, and by late 1946 the two were at war.

Ho wrote to President Truman more than once asking for support. Truman, focused on rebuilding France as a European ally, never answered.

1949–1954

China and Korea turn a colonial war into a Cold War front

The communist victory in China in 1949 and the Korean War in 1950 changed how Washington saw Vietnam. Ho was no longer a nationalist fighting a colonial power; he was the leading edge of Soviet and Chinese expansion.

So the United States started paying for France’s war, without getting any say in how France fought it.

November 1953 – May 1954

General Navarre builds a fortress in a valley

French commander Henri Navarre wanted a decisive battle. He picked a remote valley near the Laos border, Dien Bien Phu, and dared the Viet Minh to attack it.

April 1954

Eisenhower weighs an air strike

As the siege tightened, France asked for American air strikes. Inside the Eisenhower administration, the request split the people around the president.

Summer 1954

France leaves and America steps in

At Geneva, Vietnam was split at the 17th parallel, supposedly until nationwide elections in 1956. Washington decided not to rescue France, but to replace it.

  1. 1. The decision

    He refused to send bombers to save the French at Dien Bien Phu. Then he decided the United States would take France’s place in the south.

  2. 2. What followed

    The Geneva Accords split Vietnam at the 17th parallel until elections, which were set for July 1956.

  3. 3. The catch

    The United States took on the job of propping up a new, untested government in Saigon, while the fight over who should rule Vietnam stayed unsettled.

How each side saw itWhat Washington and Hanoi each wanted and assumed, what they argued about, and the pressures at home

Washington

Who decided: Harry S. Truman, Dwight D. Eisenhower, Dean Acheson, John Foster Dulles

What they wanted

Stop communism from spreading in Southeast Asia, and keep France, a NATO ally, strong enough to help rebuild Europe.

What they assumed

  • Eisenhower argued that if Indochina fell to communism, Thailand, Burma, Malaya and Indonesia would follow, one after another.

  • Washington saw Ho Chi Minh as an agent of Moscow and Beijing more than as a nationalist.

  • American money could buy a French victory, so no U.S. troops would be needed.

What they argued about

  • Secretary of State Dulles and Admiral Radford wanted carrier air strikes to save Dien Bien Phu, a plan called Operation Vulture.

  • Eisenhower and Army Chief of Staff Matthew Ridgway refused to act without Britain and Congress. Both remembered Korea.

Pressures at home

  • After Korea, the public and Congress did not want to send American ground troops to another war in Asia.

  • Senator Joseph McCarthy and others attacked leaders who seemed soft on communism. Eisenhower feared being blamed for losing Southeast Asia, as Truman had been blamed for losing China.

Hanoi

Who decided: Ho Chi Minh, Vo Nguyen Giap, Truong Chinh, Pham Van Dong

What they wanted

Drive out the French, win recognition as an independent country and build a socialist Vietnam.

What they assumed

  • A long people’s war would wear down France’s will to fight and drain its economy.

  • Chinese artillery, anti-aircraft guns and supplies, which arrived after 1950, could make up for France’s better weapons.

  • As French casualty lists grew, voters in Paris would turn against the war.

What they argued about

  • At Dien Bien Phu, Chinese advisers urged a fast, all-out assault. Giap chose a slower siege.

  • Truong Chinh’s harsh early land reform in the areas the Viet Minh controlled caused tension inside the movement.

Pressures at home

  • The Viet Minh were poor and short of food, yet they gathered about 250,000 civilian porters to carry supplies over mountain trails while French planes bombed them.

  • They depended on China and the Soviet Union for weapons and ammunition.

North Vietnamese record, Archival Diplomatic Cable (Unanswered)

Telegram from Ho Chi Minh to President Harry S. Truman

Ho Chi Minh, President, Provisional Government of the Democratic Republic of Vietnam, February 28, 1946

Demonstrates Hanoi’s early effort to cultivate American diplomatic recognition before the Cold War solidified into rigid ideological blocs in East Asia.

The exact words

“I therefore earnestly appeal to you personally and to the American people to interfere urgently in support of our independence and help making the negotiations more in keeping with the principles of the Atlantic and San Francisco charters.”

Held by: U.S. National Archives and Records Administration (NARA), Record Group 226

U.S. government record, TOP SECRET (Declassified)

The Position of the United States with Respect to Indochina (NSC 64)

National Security Council, Policy Planning Staff, Executive Office of the President, February 27, 1950

Codified what Eisenhower would later describe as the "Domino Theory," directly leading to the U.S. underwriting up to 78–80% of French military expenditures in Vietnam by 1954.

The exact words

“It is important to United States security interests that all practicable measures be taken to prevent further communist expansion in Southeast Asia. Indochina is a key area of Southeast Asia and is under immediate threat.”

Held by: Foreign Relations of the United States (FRUS), 1950, East Asia and the Pacific, Vol. VI

Cause and effectThe decision, the assumption behind it, and what it led to
  1. 1

    The decision

    President Eisenhower, May–July 1954

    He refused to send bombers to save the French at Dien Bien Phu. Then he decided the United States would take France’s place in the south.

  2. 2

    What they expected

    With France gone, Washington thought it could build a strong, independent, non-communist country in the south.

    What they got wrong.Ho Chi Minh had just beaten a European army. Millions of Vietnamese saw him as the man who won their independence.

  3. 3

    What happened

    The Geneva Accords split Vietnam at the 17th parallel until elections, which were set for July 1956.

    The response.Washington set up the SEATO alliance and backed Ngo Dinh Diem to run the south.

  4. 4

    Where it led

    The United States took on the job of propping up a new, untested government in Saigon, while the fight over who should rule Vietnam stayed unsettled.

Works cited3 works cited in this chapter

    Used for: Explains how the United States ended up paying for France’s war in Indochina, while ignoring Vietnamese who wanted independence.

  2. Fall, Bernard B. Hell in a Very Small Place: The Siege of Dien Bien Phu. Lippincott, 1966.

  3. Herring, George C. America’s Longest War: The United States and Vietnam, 1950–1975. McGraw-Hill, 2013.

    Used for: A standard history of how the United States got more and more committed to fighting communism in Southeast Asia.

    Limits: A broad overview. It says more about decisions in Washington than about the fighting itself.

Domino Theory
A Cold War idea made popular by President Eisenhower in 1954. It held that if one country fell to communism, its neighbors would fall too.
Battle of Dien Bien Phu (1954)
A 55-day siege in northwestern Vietnam. Viet Minh forces surrounded a French fortress in a valley and overran it.
Geneva Accords of 1954
The international agreement that ended the First Indochina War. It divided Vietnam at the 17th parallel for the time being, with nationwide elections set for 1956.
