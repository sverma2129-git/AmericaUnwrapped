Part 3, Chapter 10, 1973–1975, a turning point

The fall of Saigon

South Vietnam’s army was one of the best-equipped in the world on paper. Why did it collapse in just 55 days in the spring of 1975?

The 1975 Spring Offensive ended thirty years of war in Vietnam. It made the American withdrawal look like a defeat and united Vietnam under communist rule.

What happened

In 1973 and 1974, South Vietnam’s economy shrank sharply as American military spending disappeared. Congress, tired of the corruption in Saigon and set on getting out, cut military aid from $2.3 billion to less than $700 million. South Vietnamese units had to ration artillery shells and bullets, and helicopters sat on the ground for lack of spare parts. In June 1973, Congress passed the Case-Church Amendment by margins large enough to override a veto. It banned all U.S. military action in Indochina after August 15, 1973, so Nixon’s private promises of air support to Thieu could no longer be kept. In August 1974, Nixon resigned over the Watergate scandal. President Gerald Ford had neither the legal power nor the political support to step in.

In December 1974, communist forces attacked Phuoc Long province, 40 miles north of Saigon, to test whether the United States would start bombing again. Congress turned down Ford’s requests for emergency aid, and no air strikes came. Hanoi concluded that the Americans would not come back. General Van Tien Dung planned a two-year campaign for 1975 and 1976. On March 10, 1975, North Vietnamese tanks made a surprise attack and captured Ban Me Thuot in the Central Highlands. On March 14, Thieu panicked and ordered his army to abandon the whole Central Highlands so it could defend a smaller area around Saigon.

The disorganized retreat down Route 7B, an unpaved road, became known as the Column of Tears. More than 200,000 soldiers and 400,000 civilian refugees were shelled along the way, and soldiers left their posts to save their families. Hue fell on March 25. Da Nang collapsed into chaos on March 30, with 300,000 refugees crowding the city. After the war, many people believed that South Vietnamese troops simply refused to fight. That is not the whole story. At Xuan Loc, the ARVN 18th Division under Brigadier General Le Minh Dao held out from April 9 to 21 and inflicted heavy losses on three North Vietnamese divisions before it was outflanked. Hanoi sped up its timetable and renamed the push the Ho Chi Minh Campaign. At 11:30 a.m. on April 30, 1975, North Vietnamese tanks crashed through the gates of the Presidential Palace in Saigon.

The short version

After American troops left in 1973, South Vietnam’s economy sank as American military spending disappeared. Congress also cut military aid from $2.3 billion to under $700 million. ARVN units had to ration artillery shells, keep helicopters on the ground for lack of spare parts, and fight short of ammunition. In December 1974, communist forces attacked Phuoc Long province to see whether the U.S. would start bombing again. When no planes came, Hanoi approved a two-year campaign planned by General Van Tien Dung. On March 10, 1975, North Vietnamese tanks took Ban Me Thuot in the Central Highlands. In a panic, President Thieu ordered the whole Central Highlands abandoned without consulting his commanders. The retreat down the dirt road Route 7B became the “Column of Tears,” as soldiers left their units to rescue their families. Hue fell on March 25. Da Nang fell on March 30, crowded with 300,000 refugees. The ARVN 18th Division held at Xuan Loc for two weeks, but North Vietnam sped up its plans and launched the “Ho Chi Minh Campaign.” On the morning of April 30, 1975, after the rooftop helicopter evacuations of Operation Frequent Wind, North Vietnamese Tank 843 crashed through the gates of the Presidential Palace in Saigon. The war was over.

For the test

What to know

  1. Many causes. The ARVN fell apart because U.S. aid was cut, fuel and ammunition were rationed, Thieu gave panicked orders, and soldiers left to save their families.
  2. The Column of Tears. Thieu’s badly planned retreat gave up two-thirds of South Vietnam in three weeks, mostly without major battles.
  3. Operation Frequent Wind. The largest helicopter evacuation in history. Crews pushed helicopters off aircraft carriers into the sea to make room on deck.

Key terms

The Fall of Saigon (April 30, 1975)
North Vietnam’s army captured Saigon, the capital of South Vietnam. It ended the 55-day campaign that Hanoi called the Great Spring Victory.
Operation Frequent Wind (April 29–30, 1975)
The emergency helicopter evacuation of more than 1,300 Americans and 5,500 South Vietnamese at risk. Helicopters flew them from the U.S. Embassy and Tan Son Nhut Air Base to aircraft carriers offshore.
Battle of Xuan Loc (April 9–21, 1975)
The last major stand by South Vietnam’s army. The ARVN 18th Division, under Brigadier General Le Minh Dao, defended a key highway town 40 miles northeast of Saigon.

What Washington believed and what Hanoi believed

1973–1975
Who decided in WashingtonWhite House, Pentagon, CIA

What Washington wanted

Actors: Gerald R. Ford, Henry Kissinger, Graham Martin (Ambassador to Saigon)

The goal

Secure emergency congressional aid to stabilize South Vietnam, execute evacuation of American citizens, and manage the optics of defeat.

What they assumed
  • Washington assumed that even if South Vietnam declined, it would withstand major offensives through at least 1976.
  • Ambassador Graham Martin resisted early evacuation planning, fearing it would trigger instant South Vietnamese panic and accelerate the collapse.
What they argued about
  • President Ford appealed to Congress for $722 million in emergency military aid; congressional leaders flatly rejected the request, viewing the war as closed.
Who decided in HanoiPolitburo, Party Central Committee

What Hanoi wanted

Actors: Le Duan, General Van Tien Dung, Vo Nguyen Giap, Pham Hung

The goal

Liberate the South completely, crush ARVN resistance, and achieve the unconditional surrender of the Saigon government.

What they assumed
  • Hanoi initially drew up a conservative two-year campaign plan (1975–1976), expecting stubborn ARVN resistance.
  • The speed of the ARVN collapse surprised even General Dung, who issued the famous order: “Speed, greater speed; audacity, greater audacity!”
What they argued about
  • Whether to pause and consolidate supply lines after Ban Me Thuot or immediately surge all strategic reserve corps down National Route 1.

PHASE 1 OF 5March 10–12, 1975

North Vietnam breaks through at Ban Me Thuot

Sector: Ban Me Thuot (Central Highlands)
What happened on the ground:

General Van Tien Dung feigned a strike toward Pleiku and Kontum, but secretly concentrated three PAVN divisions against Ban Me Thuot. Soviet-supplied T-54 tanks and heavy artillery breached the defenses in 36 hours, bisecting South Vietnam.

Logistical & Aid Factors:

ARVN artillery was rationed to three rounds per day per tube; helicopters were grounded due to a lack of spare parts following congressional aid cuts.

Why it mattered in the 55 days:

Exposed the severe material depletion of South Vietnamese forces and shattered strategic communications between northern and southern corps.
