The Fall of Saigon

How did South Vietnam’s army collapse in just 55 days?

The fall of Saigon ended thirty years of war and reunited Vietnam under communist rule. For Americans, it made the withdrawal look like a defeat.

What happened

1973–1974

The aid runs out

In 1973 and 1974, South Vietnam’s economy shrank sharply as American military spending disappeared. Congress, tired of the corruption in Saigon and set on getting out, cut military aid from $2.3 billion to less than $700 million. South Vietnamese units had to ration artillery shells and bullets, and helicopters sat on the ground for lack of spare parts. In June 1973, Congress passed the Case-Church Amendment by margins large enough to override a veto. It banned all U.S. military action in Indochina after August 15, 1973, so Nixon’s private promises of air support to Thieu could no longer be kept. In August 1974, Nixon resigned over the Watergate scandal. President Gerald Ford had neither the legal power nor the political support to step in.

December 1974 – March 1975

Hanoi tests Washington

In December 1974, communist forces attacked Phuoc Long province, about 60 to 75 miles north of Saigon, to test whether the United States would start bombing again. Congress turned down Ford’s requests for emergency aid, and no air strikes came. Hanoi concluded that the Americans would not come back. General Van Tien Dung planned a two-year campaign for 1975 and 1976. On March 10, 1975, North Vietnamese tanks made a surprise attack and captured Ban Me Thuot in the Central Highlands. On March 14, Thieu panicked and ordered his army to abandon the whole Central Highlands so it could defend a smaller area around Saigon.

March–April 1975

Fifty-five days

The disorganized retreat down Route 7B, an unpaved road, became known as the Column of Tears. More than 200,000 soldiers and 400,000 civilian refugees were shelled along the way, and soldiers left their posts to save their families. Hue fell on March 25. Da Nang collapsed into chaos on March 30, with 300,000 refugees crowding the city. After the war, many people believed that South Vietnamese troops simply refused to fight. That is not the whole story. At Xuan Loc, the ARVN 18th Division under Brigadier General Le Minh Dao held out from April 9 to 21 and inflicted heavy losses on three North Vietnamese divisions before it was outflanked. Hanoi sped up its timetable and renamed the push the Ho Chi Minh Campaign. At 11:30 a.m. on April 30, 1975, North Vietnamese tanks crashed through the gates of the Presidential Palace in Saigon.

The short version

After American troops left in 1973, South Vietnam’s economy sank as American military spending disappeared. Congress also cut military aid from $2.3 billion to under $700 million. ARVN units had to ration artillery shells, keep helicopters on the ground for lack of spare parts, and fight short of ammunition. In December 1974, communist forces attacked Phuoc Long province to see whether the U.S. would start bombing again. When no planes came, Hanoi approved a two-year campaign planned by General Van Tien Dung. On March 10, 1975, North Vietnamese tanks took Ban Me Thuot in the Central Highlands. In a panic, President Thieu ordered the whole Central Highlands abandoned without consulting his commanders. The retreat down the dirt road Route 7B became the Column of Tears, as soldiers left their units to rescue their families. Hue fell on March 25. Da Nang fell on March 30, crowded with 300,000 refugees. The ARVN 18th Division held at Xuan Loc for two weeks, but North Vietnam sped up its plans and launched the Ho Chi Minh Campaign. On the morning of April 30, 1975, after the rooftop helicopter evacuations of Operation Frequent Wind, North Vietnamese Tank 843 crashed through the gates of the Presidential Palace in Saigon. The war was over.

    The decision

    Congress; President Thieu; General Van Tien Dung, 1973–1975

    Congress cut aid and barred any return of American air power. Thieu ordered the retreat from the Central Highlands, and Hanoi sped up its attack.

  2. 2

    What they expected

    Thieu thought his army could pull back in good order and defend a smaller area. Congress thought cutting aid would push Saigon toward a political deal.

    What they got wrong.An army retreating under fire, with soldiers’ families on the same roads, broke into a rout. The aid cuts had already left units short of fuel and ammunition.

  3. 3

    What happened

    South Vietnam fell in 55 days. The end came on April 30, 1975.

    The response.General Duong Van Minh, president for two days, announced South Vietnam’s unconditional surrender.

    Where it led

    The war ended in total defeat for the South, and hundreds of thousands of people soon fled the country.

What each side wantedWashington and Hanoi, judged by the same questions

Washington

Who decided: Gerald R. Ford, Henry Kissinger, Graham Martin (Ambassador to Saigon)

What they wanted

Win emergency aid from Congress to steady South Vietnam, get Americans out safely and limit the damage of defeat.

What they assumed

  • Even if South Vietnam was weakening, Washington expected it to hold out against major attacks until at least 1976.

  • Ambassador Graham Martin put off evacuation plans because he feared they would set off panic and speed the collapse.

What they argued about

  • President Ford asked Congress for $722 million in emergency military aid. Congressional leaders turned him down, treating the war as finished.

Pressures at home

  • Americans and Congress did not want to lose more soldiers or spend more money in Southeast Asia.

Hanoi

Who decided: Le Duan, General Van Tien Dung, Vo Nguyen Giap, Pham Hung

What they wanted

Take the whole South, defeat South Vietnam’s army and force Saigon to surrender without conditions.

What they assumed

  • At first Hanoi planned a two-year campaign for 1975 and 1976, expecting South Vietnam’s army to fight hard.

  • The collapse came so fast that it surprised even General Dung, who ordered his commanders to move faster and take bigger risks.

What they argued about

  • After Ban Me Thuot fell, commanders debated whether to pause and bring up supplies, or send every reserve unit down Route 1 at once.

Pressures at home

  • Supply lines stretched thin as hundreds of trucks and tanks, and fuel pipelines, moved south over hundreds of miles of captured highway.

Agreement on Ending the War and Restoring Peace in Viet-Nam (Paris Accords)

William P. Rogers, Nguyen Duy Trinh, Tran Van Lam, Nguyen Thi Binh, Signatories representing USA, DRV (North Vietnam), RVN (South Vietnam), PRG (Viet Cong), January 27, 1973

President Thieu viewed the treaty as a fatal compromise for South Vietnam, acquiescing only after Nixon secretly promised in personal letters that the U.S. would "respond with full force" if Hanoi violated the agreement—a commitment Congress soon rendered legally impossible via the Case-Church Amendment.

Step 1 of 5, March 10–12, 1975

North Vietnam breaks through at Ban Me Thuot

General Van Tien Dung faked an attack toward Pleiku and Kontum, then massed three divisions against Ban Me Thuot. Soviet-supplied tanks and heavy artillery broke through in 36 hours and cut South Vietnam in two.

Supplies and aid
After the aid cuts, South Vietnamese gunners were limited to three shells a day per gun, and helicopters sat grounded for lack of spare parts.
Why it mattered
The attack showed how short of supplies South Vietnam’s army had become, and it cut the links between its northern and southern commands.
