The Central Inquiry

Why did the South Vietnamese military—on paper one of the best-equipped armies in the world—collapse in just 55 days in the spring of 1975?

Why It Matters: The 1975 Spring Offensive marks the end of the thirty-year Vietnam War, the final humiliation of the American withdrawal, and the unification of Vietnam under communist rule.

What Happened in Brief

Following the 1973 American withdrawal, South Vietnam’s economy cratered as military expenditures vanished. Simultaneously, a disillusioned U.S. Congress slashed military aid from $2.3 billion to under $700 million, forcing ARVN units to ration artillery shells, ground helicopters for lack of spare parts, and fight with scarce ammunition. In December 1974, communist forces attacked Phuoc Long province to test whether the U.S. would resume bombing; when no strikes occurred, Hanoi authorized a two-year campaign planned by General Van Tien Dung. On March 10, 1975, PAVN armor captured Ban Me Thuot in the Central Highlands. In panic, President Thieu ordered the entire Central Highlands abandoned without consultation, turning the retreat down unpaved Route 7B into the disastrous "Column of Tears" where soldiers abandoned units to rescue fleeing families. Hue fell on March 25; Da Nang collapsed on March 30 amid 300,000 refugees. Despite a heroic two-week stand by the ARVN 18th Division at Xuan Loc, North Vietnamese forces accelerated their timetable into the "Ho Chi Minh Campaign." On the morning of April 30, 1975, following the frantic rooftop helicopter evacuations of Operation Frequent Wind, North Vietnamese Tank 843 rammed through the gates of Saigon’s Presidential Palace, ending the war.

Key Exam Takeaways (AP • State Standards)
  • 1.Multi-Causal Collapse: The ARVN collapse was caused by a combination of severe U.S. aid reductions, fuel/ammo rationing, strategic panic orders from President Thieu, and the psychological collapse of soldier family networks.
  • 2.The Column of Tears: Thieu’s disastrous uncoordinated retreat order surrendered two-thirds of South Vietnam in three weeks without major pitched combat.
  • 3.Operation Frequent Wind: The largest helicopter evacuation in history, capturing the iconic image of helicopters being pushed over the side of aircraft carriers into the South China Sea to clear flight decks.

Section 02 • The Historical Narrative

How Events Unfolded

Carefully researched narrative grounded in primary sources and declassified records.

Between 1973 and 1974, South Vietnam’s economy contracted severely as American military expenditures vanished. Simultaneously, the U.S. Congress, disillusioned by corruption and determined to conclude involvement, slashed military aid from $2.3 billion to under $700 million. ARVN units were forced to ration artillery shells, ground helicopters for lack of spare parts, and conserve ammunition. In June 1973, Congress passed the Case-Church Amendment with veto-proof majorities, prohibiting all U.S. military activity in Indochina past August 15, 1973, legally voiding Nixon’s private promises of air support to Thieu. In August 1974, Nixon resigned over the Watergate scandal, leaving President Gerald Ford with neither the authority nor the political capital to intervene.

In December 1974, communist forces attacked Phuoc Long province, 40 miles north of Saigon, specifically to test whether the U.S. would resume bombing. When Congress rejected Ford’s appeals for emergency assistance and no air strikes occurred, Hanoi concluded the U.S. would not return. General Van Tien Dung planned a two-year campaign for 1975–1976. On March 10, 1975, PAVN armor launched a surprise assault capturing Ban Me Thuot in the Central Highlands. On March 14, in strategic panic, President Thieu ordered the entire Central Highlands abandoned to consolidate a defensible enclave around Saigon.

The uncoordinated retreat down unpaved Route 7B degenerated into the "Column of Tears": over 200,000 soldiers and 400,000 civilian refugees were shelled along the roads, and soldiers abandoned positions to save their fleeing families. Hue fell on March 25; Da Nang collapsed into pandemonium on March 30 amid 300,000 refugees. Contrary to the post-war stereotype that South Vietnamese troops universally refused to fight, the ARVN 18th Division under Brigadier General Le Minh Dao mounted a fierce two-week stand at Xuan Loc (April 9–21), inflicting heavy casualties on three PAVN divisions before being outflanked. Accelerating the timetable into the "Ho Chi Minh Campaign," North Vietnamese armor crashed through the gates of Saigon’s Presidential Palace at 11:30 AM on April 30, 1975.

“On the morning of April 30, 1975, North Vietnamese armored units breached the perimeter gates of Saigon’s Presidential Palace, concluding the military collapse of the Republic of Vietnam.”
— George J. Veith, Historical account based on Black April (2012) (April 30, 1975 &bull; 11:30 AM)

Causal Anatomy • Strategy & Consequence

The Chain of Consequence

Decision → Blind Spot → Reaction → Fallout
I. The Decision1973–1975
Actor: U.S. Congress / President Thieu / General Van Tien Dung

Congress legally barred air re-intervention and cut aid; Thieu ordered the Central Highlands retreat; Hanoi accelerated its offensive.

II. The Blind Spot
Premise:

Thieu assumed ARVN could execute an orderly retreat to form an enclave; Congress assumed aid cuts would encourage political accommodation.

Unexamined Risk:

A retreat under fire by an army accompanied by panic-stricken families dissolved into a rout; aid cuts crippled operational readiness.

III. The Collision
Immediate Tactical Event:

The complete collapse of South Vietnam in 55 days, concluding on April 30, 1975.

Adversary Counter:

General Duong Van Minh announced unconditional surrender in Saigon.

IV. Strategic Consequence

Thirty years of warfare ended not in a negotiated settlement, but in total military collapse, initiating massive refugee migrations and regional instability.

The Strategic Alignment • 1973–1975

What Washington Believed vs. What Hanoi Believed

Washington Decision-MakersWhite House • Pentagon • CIA

The American Imperative

Actors: Gerald R. Ford, Henry Kissinger, Graham Martin (Ambassador to Saigon)

Strategic Objective

Secure emergency congressional aid to stabilize South Vietnam, execute evacuation of American citizens, and manage the optics of defeat.

Underlying Strategic Premises
  • Washington assumed that even if South Vietnam declined, it would withstand major offensives through at least 1976.
  • Ambassador Graham Martin resisted early evacuation planning, fearing it would trigger instant South Vietnamese panic and accelerate the collapse.
Internal Dissent & Policy Debate
  • • President Ford appealed to Congress for $722 million in emergency military aid; congressional leaders flatly rejected the request, viewing the war as closed.
Hanoi Decision-MakersPolitburo • VWP Central Committee

The Revolutionary Imperative

Actors: Le Duan, General Van Tien Dung, Vo Nguyen Giap, Pham Hung

Strategic Objective

Liberate the South completely, crush ARVN resistance, and achieve the unconditional surrender of the Saigon government.

Underlying Revolutionary Premises
  • Hanoi initially drew up a conservative two-year campaign plan (1975–1976), expecting stubborn ARVN resistance.
  • The speed of the ARVN collapse surprised even General Dung, who issued the famous order: "Speed, greater speed; audacity, greater audacity!"
Internal Purges & Tactical Factions
  • • Whether to pause and consolidate supply lines after Ban Me Thuot or immediately surge all strategic reserve corps down National Route 1.

Campaign Cartography• The 55-Day Spring 1975 Collapse
PHASE 1 OF 5March 10–12, 1975

The Breakthrough at Ban Me Thuot

Sector: Ban Me Thuot (Central Highlands)
Operational Realities:

General Van Tien Dung feigned a strike toward Pleiku and Kontum, but secretly concentrated three PAVN divisions against Ban Me Thuot. Soviet-supplied T-54 tanks and heavy artillery breached the defenses in 36 hours, bisecting South Vietnam.

Logistical & Aid Factors:

ARVN artillery was rationed to three rounds per day per tube; helicopters were grounded due to a lack of spare parts following congressional aid cuts.

Significance in the 55-Day Arc:

Exposed the severe material depletion of South Vietnamese forces and shattered strategic communications between northern and southern corps.
