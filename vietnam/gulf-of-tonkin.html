Part 2, Chapter 41964A turning point

The Gulf of Tonkin

How did an attack that most likely never happened lead America into war?

Congress voted 504 to 2 to let Johnson use force. For nine years that resolution did the work of a declaration of war.

  • The story
  • Sources
  • For the test

The short version

  • There were two incidents. On August 2, North Vietnamese torpedo boats really did attack the Maddox. The August 4 attack came from radar and sonar reports, and it most likely never happened.

  • The Gulf of Tonkin Resolution let Johnson take all necessary measures to repel armed attack. Congress never declared war.

  • In 1973 Congress passed the War Powers Act over Nixon’s veto. A president now has to report troop deployments within 48 hours and get approval within 60 days.

What happened

The story in six short parts. A few end with a question to check yourself.

January–July 1964

A secret war along the coast

In early 1964, the United States began backing secret raids on North Vietnam’s coast. Under a plan called OPLAN 34A, South Vietnamese commandos struck radar sites and islands.

At the same time, U.S. Navy destroyers sailed the Gulf of Tonkin on spy missions called DeSoto patrols. They recorded North Vietnam’s radio and radar signals. On July 31, commandos shelled Hon Me Island while the destroyer Maddox was offshore. To Hanoi, the raids and the patrols looked like one operation.

August 2, 1964

A real fight in daylight

On the afternoon of August 2, three North Vietnamese torpedo boats raced toward the Maddox. The American commander on board sent a warning and got ready to fire.

In the fight that followed, planes from the carrier Ticonderoga damaged the boats. The Maddox was hit by a single machine-gun bullet.

Night of August 4, 1964

A night of confusion

Two nights later, the Maddox and a second destroyer, the Turner Joy, were back in the gulf. A storm rolled in after dark. Then the radar screens lit up.

August 2 vs. August 4

The two nights compared

Historians agree on what happened on August 2. Using records released decades later, they also agree that the August 4 attack most likely never happened.

Check yourself

What was the key difference between the August 2 and August 4, 1964, incidents in the Gulf of Tonkin?

August 4–7, 1964

Washington acts fast

Johnson ordered air strikes on North Vietnamese naval bases, called Operation Pierce Arrow. He was also running for president against Barry Goldwater, who called the administration weak.

Three days later, Congress passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution. It let the president use force to repel attacks and prevent further aggression, without a declaration of war.

Check yourself

1965–1973

Congress hands over its war power, then takes it back

The resolution did not declare war, but it worked like one. When Viet Cong forces attacked U.S. barracks at Pleiku in February 1965, Johnson already had the power to escalate. Within weeks, steady bombing began and U.S. combat troops landed.

Years later, Congress tried to take that power back. The War Powers Resolution of 1973 says a president can send troops into combat only after a declaration of war, a specific law, or an attack on the United States. It passed over President Nixon’s veto.

  1. 1. The decision

    Congress passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution after the administration presented doubtful reports of a second attack as fact.

  2. 2. What followed

    Johnson won the November 1964 election in a landslide, holding open-ended legal authority to use force.

  3. 3. The catch

    When the Viet Cong attacked the U.S. base at Pleiku in February 1965, Johnson already had the power to escalate and few easy ways to back down.

Check yourself

Sources

Every claim in the story comes from something in this section.

Historians disagree: Did the Johnson administration mislead Congress, or rush to judgment?

Historians now agree that no attack took place on August 4. They disagree about why Washington acted as if one had.

A. A rush to judgment

Officials acted fast on confused early reports. The worst errors came from how intelligence was handled, not from a plan to lie.

The evidence for it

  • Moise’s reconstruction does not claim that Johnson knowingly lied.

  • The NSA study blamed mid-level analysts who filtered intercepts, not top officials.

  • McNamara later called it confusion and admitted the attack did not happen.

B. An opportunity seized

The administration wanted broad authority for a wider war and used the incident to get it.

The evidence for it

  • The text of a congressional resolution had been drafted months before August 1964.

  • Herrick’s doubts reached Washington about ten hours before the president spoke.

  • Johnson was campaigning against Goldwater, who called the administration weak.

How each side saw itWhat Washington and Hanoi each wanted and assumed, what they argued about, and the pressures at home

Washington

Who decided: Lyndon B. Johnson, Robert S. McNamara, Dean Rusk, Adm. U.S. Grant Sharp Jr., Sen. Wayne Morse

What they wanted

Show Hanoi that America meant business, steady the Saigon government and answer Barry Goldwater’s charge that Johnson was weak.

What they assumed

  • A sharp show of American air power would convince Hanoi that backing the southern guerrillas cost too much.

  • Congress could hand the president broad power to use force, to be held in reserve, without a declaration of war or a long debate.

What they argued about

  • McNamara and the military relied on radio intercepts that seemed to confirm an attack, and set aside Captain Herrick’s cables asking them to wait.

  • Senators Wayne Morse and Ernest Gruening warned that the resolution gave away Congress’s war power on the basis of unchecked claims.

Pressures at home

  • The 1964 presidential campaign: Johnson campaigned as the prudent candidate against Goldwater, promising not to send “American boys 9 or 10,000 miles away from home to do what Asian boys ought to be doing for themselves.”

Hanoi

Who decided: Le Duan, Ho Chi Minh, General Vo Nguyen Giap, Nguyen Chi Thanh

What they wanted

Defend the coast against the OPLAN 34A raids and keep supplies moving to the guerrillas in the South.

What they assumed

  • The American spy patrols and the South Vietnamese commando raids were one operation against North Vietnam.

  • An open American war was coming whether or not Hanoi held back.

What they argued about

  • On August 2, local naval commanders attacked because they believed the Maddox was guiding the commando boats.

  • Hanoi said its boats took part in no fighting on the night of August 4.

Pressures at home

  • Hanoi built civil defense networks and dug bomb shelters along the sidewalks to get ready for American bombing.

The Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (Joint Resolution of Congress, Public Law 88-408)

88th United States Congress, Legislative Branch of the United States, August 7, 1964

Served as the functional legal authorization utilized by the Johnson administration to conduct a major, undeclared ground and air war in Southeast Asia.

The exact words

“Resolved by the Senate and House of Representatives of the United States of America in Congress assembled, That the Congress approves and supports the determination of the President, as Commander in Chief, to take all necessary measures to repel any armed attack against the forces of the United States and to prevent further aggression.”

Held by: U.S. National Archives Milestone Documents / 78 Stat. 384

U.S. government record, TOP SECRET // COMINT-CHANNELS (Declassified)

Cryptologic Analysis: "Skunks, Bogies, Silent Hounds, and the Flying Fish"

Robert J. Hanyok, Historian, National Security Agency (Center for Cryptologic History), Winter 2000 / Spring 2001 (Declassified 2005)

Proves through official intelligence records that the August 4 incident was not simply confused sailors in a storm, but an instance where ambiguous signals intelligence was selectively summarized upstream to fit an established administration objective.

The exact words

“First, it is not simply that there is a different story as to what happened; it is that no attack happened that night.”

Held by: National Security Archive Electronic Briefing Book No. 132 / NSA Cryptologic Quarterly

Cause and effectThe decision, the assumption behind it, and what it led to
  1. 1

    The decision

    Lyndon Johnson and Congress, August 7, 1964

    Congress passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution after the administration presented doubtful reports of a second attack as fact.

  2. 2

    What they expected

    Johnson saw the resolution as a warning to Hanoi and political cover in an election year. He thought it would help prevent a wider war.

    What they got wrong.Hanoi read the American air strikes as proof that a full war was coming, and got ready for one.

Works cited3 works cited in this chapter

  1. Moise, Edwin E. Tonkin Gulf and the Escalation of the Vietnam War. University of North Carolina Press, 1996.

    Used for: A detailed reconstruction of the night of August 4, showing there were no North Vietnamese boats there and that bad radar and sonar readings caused the reports.

    Limits: Moise does not say Johnson knowingly lied. He says officials acted too fast on reports that were wrong.

  2. Hanyok, Robert J. "Skunks, Bogies, Silent Hounds, and the Flying Fish: The Gulf of Tonkin Mystery." Cryptologic Quarterly, NSA, 2001.

    Used for: The National Security Agency’s own history of the Gulf of Tonkin. It found that the intercepted messages used as proof of an August 4 attack were really about the August 2 fight.

    Limits: It blames analysts who passed along only the evidence that fit, not top officials knowingly making up an attack.

  3. Logevall, Fredrik. Choosing War: The Lost Chance for Peace and the Escalation of War in Vietnam. University of California Press, 1999.

    Used for: Shows that the Johnson administration had written a draft resolution months before the Gulf of Tonkin and used the incident to get it passed.

    Limits: About political choices in Washington and missed chances for peace, not what happened at sea.

Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (1964)
A joint resolution of Congress. It authorized President Johnson to take all necessary measures to repel armed attack on U.S. forces and to prevent further aggression.
OPLAN 34A
A secret operation run by the CIA and the U.S. military command. South Vietnamese commandos in unmarked boats raided North Vietnam’s coast.
DeSoto Patrols
Spy missions by U.S. Navy destroyers off communist coasts. The ships recorded radar sites and radio signals.
War Powers Act of 1973
A federal law passed over President Nixon’s veto. The president must tell Congress within 48 hours of sending troops into action, and must withdraw them after 60 days unless Congress approves.
