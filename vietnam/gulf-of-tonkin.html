The Gulf of Tonkin: Two Nights and a Resolution

Two sea incidents, selective intelligence, and the congressional resolution for war

Chapter 4 · 1964 · Turning point

I’m studying for

  2. 2. The story (3 minutes)
  3. 3. The evidence (go deeper)
  4. 4. Practice (for the test)

What really happened in the Gulf of Tonkin in August 1964, and how did it lead to a much larger American war?

  1. 1. Dual Incidents

    August 2 was a confirmed daylight firefight; August 4 was a phantom radar battle that never occurred.

  2. 2. Blank Check Resolution

    The Tonkin Gulf Resolution granted LBJ all necessary measures to repel armed attack, bypassing the constitutional requirement for Congress to declare war.

  3. 3. War Powers Act of 1973

    Passed over Nixon’s veto in direct legislative reaction to Tonkin, requiring presidents to report troop deployments within 48 hours and obtain approval within 60 days.

How it happened

6 short parts, about 3 minutes. A few end with a quick check.

A secret war along the coast

January–July 1964

In early 1964, the United States began backing secret raids on North Vietnam’s coast. Under a plan called OPLAN 34A, South Vietnamese commandos struck radar sites and islands.

At the same time, U.S. Navy destroyers sailed the Gulf of Tonkin on spy missions called DeSoto patrols. They recorded North Vietnam’s radio and radar signals. On July 31, commandos shelled Hon Me Island while the destroyer Maddox was offshore. To Hanoi, the raids and the patrols looked like one operation.

A real fight in daylight

August 2, 1964

On the afternoon of August 2, three North Vietnamese torpedo boats raced toward the Maddox. The American commander on board sent a warning and got ready to fire.

In the fight that followed, planes from the carrier Ticonderoga damaged the boats. The Maddox was hit by a single machine-gun bullet.

A night of confusion

Night of August 4, 1964

Two nights later, the Maddox and a second destroyer, the Turner Joy, were back in the gulf. A storm rolled in after dark. Then the radar screens lit up.

Quick check. What did Task Group Commander Captain John Herrick report to Washington hours after the August 4 incident?

Two nights, two different stories

August 2 vs. August 4

Historians agree on what happened on August 2. Using records released decades later, they also agree that the August 4 attack most likely never happened.

Quick check. What was the critical difference between the naval incidents in the Gulf of Tonkin on August 2 and August 4, 1964?

Washington acts fast

August 4–7, 1964

Johnson ordered air strikes on North Vietnamese naval bases, called Operation Pierce Arrow. He was also running for president against Barry Goldwater, who called the administration weak.

Three days later, Congress passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution. It let the president use force to repel attacks and prevent further aggression, without a declaration of war.

Quick check. How did Congress respond to President Johnson’s presentation of the Tonkin incident?

A blank check, then a correction

1965–1973

The resolution did not declare war, but it worked like one. When Viet Cong forces attacked U.S. barracks at Pleiku in February 1965, Johnson already had the power to escalate. Within weeks, steady bombing began and U.S. combat troops landed.

Years later, Congress tried to take that power back. The War Powers Resolution of 1973 says a president can send troops into combat only after a declaration of war, a specific law, or an attack on the United States. It passed over President Nixon’s veto.

  1. 1. The decision

    Passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution based on selectively presented intelligence of a second naval engagement.

  2. 2. What followed

    LBJ won the 1964 presidential election by a historic landslide; the resolution provided legal authority for open-ended military action.

  3. 3. The catch

    When the Viet Cong attacked American barracks at Pleiku in February 1965, LBJ had a blank check in hand and few diplomatic off-ramps remaining.

Quick check. Which 1973 law was enacted by Congress specifically to prevent future undeclared military interventions like the one authorized by the Tonkin Resolution?

The evidence

Everything above is backed by what is in here. Open whatever you are curious about.

Historians disagree: Did the Johnson administration mislead Congress, or rush to judgment?

Historians now agree that no attack took place on August 4. They disagree about why Washington acted as if one had.

A. A rush to judgment

Officials acted fast on confused early reports. The worst errors came from how intelligence was handled, not from a plan to lie.

The evidence for it

  • Moise’s reconstruction does not claim that Johnson knowingly lied.

  • The NSA study blamed mid-level analysts who filtered intercepts, not top officials.

  • McNamara later called it confusion and admitted the attack did not happen.

B. An opportunity seized

The administration wanted broad authority for a wider war and used the incident to get it.

The evidence for it

  • The text of a congressional resolution had been drafted months before August 1964.

  • Herrick’s doubts reached Washington about ten hours before the president spoke.

  • Johnson was campaigning against Goldwater, who called the administration weak.

  1. “AM BEING APPROACHED BY HIGH SPEED CRAFT WITH APPARENT INTENTION OF TORPEDO ATTACK. INTEND TO OPEN FIRE IF NECESSARY SELF DEFENSE.”

    Commander Task Group 72.1 / USS Maddox, Operational Flash Message DTG 020640Z AUG 64

    Primary source, August 2, 1964

    What it can’t tell you: Pertains strictly to the confirmed August 2 daylight engagement, which was distinct from the disputed night action of August 4.

    Ask yourself: Who created this, and when? Is it a primary source (from the time) or a secondary source (written later)?

  2. “Review of action makes many reported contacts and torpedoes fired appear doubtful. Freak weather effects on radar and overeager sonarmen may have accounted for many reports. No actual visual sightings by Maddox. Suggest complete evaluation before any further action taken.”

    Captain John J. Herrick (Commander, Task Group 72.1), Flash Telegram 041727Z AUG 64 from CTG 72.1 to CINCPACFLT

    Primary source, August 4, 1964 (1:27 PM EDT)

    What it can’t tell you: Does not establish that naval commanders knew with certainty no attack had occurred; documents tactical ambiguity, sonar confusion, and a recommendation for caution.

    Ask yourself: Who created this, and when? Is it a primary source (from the time) or a secondary source (written later)?

  3. “I had the best seat in the house to watch that event, and our destroyers were just shooting at phantom targets—there were no PT boats there... There was nothing there but black water and American firepower.”

    Commander James B. Stockdale, In Love and War: The Story of a Family’s Ordeal and Sacrifice During the Vietnam Years

    Secondary source, 1984

    What it can’t tell you: Memoir recollection written twenty years after the engagement; corroborates Herrick’s contemporaneous doubts but reflects individual pilot visibility in severe weather.

    Ask yourself: Who created this, and when? Is it a primary source (from the time) or a secondary source (written later)?

  4. “But repeated acts of violence against the Armed Forces of the United States must be met not only with alert defense, but with positive reply. That reply is being given as I speak to you tonight.”

    President Lyndon B. Johnson, Radio and Television Report Following Renewed Aggression in the Gulf of Tonkin

    Primary source, August 4, 1964 (began 11:36 p.m. EDT)

    What it can’t tell you: States the administration’s account of August 4; it does not reflect the on-scene doubts Captain Herrick had cabled that afternoon.

    Ask yourself: Who created this, and when? Is it a primary source (from the time) or a secondary source (written later)?

  5. “Resolved by the Senate and House of Representatives of the United States of America in Congress assembled, That the Congress approves and supports the determination of the President, as Commander in Chief, to take all necessary measures to repel any armed attack against the forces of the United States and to prevent further aggression.”

    88th United States Congress, The Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (Public Law 88-408)

    Primary source, August 7, 1964

    What it can’t tell you: Passed 416–0 in the House and 88–2 in the Senate based on administration assurances that naval forces had been subjected to unprovoked attacks.

    Ask yourself: Who created this, and when? Is it a primary source (from the time) or a secondary source (written later)?

  6. “I believe that history will record that we have made a great mistake in subverting and circumventing the Constitution of the United States. . . I believe this resolution to be a historic mistake.”

    Senator Wayne Morse (D-OR), Speech on the Senate Floor Debating the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution

    Primary source, August 7, 1964

    What it can’t tell you: Morse and Senator Gruening were the only two senators who voted against the resolution; 88 senators voted in favor.

    Ask yourself: Who created this, and when? Is it a primary source (from the time) or a secondary source (written later)?

  7. “But we are not about to send American boys 9 or 10,000 miles away from home to do what Asian boys ought to be doing for themselves.”

    President Lyndon B. Johnson, Remarks in Memorial Hall, Akron University

    Primary source, October 21, 1964

    What it can’t tell you: A campaign speech meant to contrast Johnson with Barry Goldwater; it is not a statement of internal policy planning.

    Ask yourself: Who created this, and when? Is it a primary source (from the time) or a secondary source (written later)?

  8. “First, it is not simply that there is a different story as to what happened; it is that no attack happened that night.”

    Robert J. Hanyok (NSA Center for Cryptologic History), Skunks, Bogies, Silent Hounds, and the Flying Fish: The Gulf of Tonkin Mystery, 2–4 August 1964

    Later analysis, Winter 2000 / Spring 2001 (Declassified 2005)

    What it can’t tell you: Concluded that mid-level analysts filtered data to support the attack hypothesis, not that high-level administration officials directed intelligence falsification.

    Ask yourself: Who created this, and when? Is it a primary source (from the time) or a secondary source (written later)?

  9. “It was just confusion, and events afterward showed that our judgment that we’d been attacked that day was wrong. It didn’t happen.”

    Robert S. McNamara, The Fog of War: Eleven Lessons from the Life of Robert S. McNamara

    Later analysis, 2003

    What it can’t tell you: Retrospective interview recorded four decades after the events; reflects McNamara’s retrospective admission rather than 1964 contemporaneous deliberation.

    Ask yourself: Who created this, and when? Is it a primary source (from the time) or a secondary source (written later)?

  10. “The constitutional powers of the President as Commander-in-Chief to introduce United States Armed Forces into hostilities, or into situations where imminent involvement in hostilities is clearly indicated by the circumstances, are exercised only pursuant to (1) a declaration of war, (2) specific statutory authorization, or (3) a national emergency created by attack upon the United States, its territories or possessions, or its armed forces.”

    93rd United States Congress, War Powers Resolution, Section 2(c)

    Primary source, November 7, 1973

    What it can’t tell you: Passed over President Nixon’s veto; later presidents have questioned its constitutionality and observed it unevenly.

    Ask yourself: Who created this, and when? Is it a primary source (from the time) or a secondary source (written later)?

How each side saw itWhat Washington and Hanoi each wanted, assumed, and argued about

What Washington Believed vs. What Hanoi Believed

The Strategic Alignment • 1964
Washington Decision-MakersWhite House • Pentagon • CIA

The American Imperative

Actors: Lyndon B. Johnson, Robert S. McNamara, Dean Rusk, Adm. U.S. Grant Sharp Jr., Sen. Wayne Morse

Strategic Objective

Demonstrate American resolve to deter Hanoi, stabilize the Saigon government, and neutralize Barry Goldwater’s charges of administration weakness during the 1964 presidential campaign.

Underlying Strategic Premises
  • A targeted display of American airpower would convince Hanoi that the cost of pursuing the southern insurgency was prohibitive.
  • Broad congressional war-making authority could be obtained and held in reserve without requiring a formal declaration of war or extended public debate.
Internal Dissent & Policy Debate
  • • McNamara and military commanders relied on selectively summarized SIGINT intercepts that appeared to confirm an attack, dismissing Captain Herrick’s urgent cables urging delay.
  • • Senators Wayne Morse and Ernest Gruening warned that the resolution was an unconstitutional transfer of war power based on unverified allegations.[^Q022]
Hanoi Decision-MakersPolitburo • VWP Central Committee

The Revolutionary Imperative

Actors: Le Duan, Ho Chi Minh, General Vo Nguyen Giap, Nguyen Chi Thanh

Strategic Objective

Defend coastal territorial waters against OPLAN 34A raids and maintain supply lines to the southern insurgency.

Underlying Revolutionary Premises
  • U.S. naval intelligence patrols and South Vietnamese commando raids were an indivisible joint operation violating DRV sovereignty.
  • Open American military escalation was increasingly probable regardless of North Vietnamese restraint.
Internal Purges & Tactical Factions
  • • Local naval commanders acted aggressively on August 2, believing the Maddox was directly guiding the 34A raiding boats.
  • • Hanoi confirmed that its naval vessels did not participate in any engagement on the night of August 4.

The Chain of Consequence

Decision Blind Spot Reaction Fallout
I. The DecisionAugust 7, 1964
Actor: Johnson Administration & U.S. Congress

Passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution based on selectively presented intelligence of a second naval engagement.

II. The Blind Spot
Premise:

The resolution was a deterrent tool and political insurance policy that would prevent war rather than initiate it.

Unexamined Risk:

Hanoi interpreted the retaliatory airstrikes not as a deterrent, but as proof that direct American war was inevitable, triggering preemptive escalation.

III. The Collision
Immediate Tactical Event:

LBJ won the 1964 presidential election by a historic landslide; the resolution provided legal authority for open-ended military action.

Adversary Counter:

Hanoi dispatched the first full regular PAVN infantry regiments south down the Ho Chi Minh Trail.

IV. Strategic Consequence

When the Viet Cong attacked American barracks at Pleiku in February 1965, LBJ had a blank check in hand and few diplomatic off-ramps remaining.

Every source we used3 works cited in this chapter

  1. Moise, Edwin E. Tonkin Gulf and the Escalation of the Vietnam War. University of North Carolina Press, 1996.

    Used for: Establishes the definitive naval reconstruction of August 4, proving that no North Vietnamese boats were present and that radar/sonar anomalies drove reported contacts.

    Limits: Technical tactical reconstruction; does not claim Johnson knowingly lied on August 4, emphasizing instead that officials rushed to act on premature and flawed reports.

  2. Hanyok, Robert J. "Skunks, Bogies, Silent Hounds, and the Flying Fish: The Gulf of Tonkin Mystery." Cryptologic Quarterly, NSA, 2001.

    Used for: Official internal NSA historical study demonstrating that communications intercepts cited as evidence of an attack on August 4 actually pertained to salvage operations from August 2.

    Limits: Concluded that mid-level SIGINT analysts filtered data to support the attack hypothesis, not that high-level administration officials directed intelligence falsification.

  3. Logevall, Fredrik. Choosing War: The Lost Chance for Peace and the Escalation of War in Vietnam. University of California Press, 1999.

    Used for: Demonstrates that the Johnson administration had prepared the text of a congressional resolution months before Tonkin and seized upon the incident to secure legislative authority.

    Limits: Focuses on executive political strategy and diplomatic lost opportunities rather than naval radar mechanics.
