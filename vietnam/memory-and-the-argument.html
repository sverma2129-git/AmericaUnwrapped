Chapter 12, The Argument

How historians argue about the war

Four ways of explaining Vietnam, and the evidence behind each

Was the Vietnam War a quagmire that could never be won, or a war the U.S. could have won if politicians and the press had not gotten in the way?

Historians still argue about this, and the two sides are called "orthodox" and "revisionist." Their answers still shape how Americans think about going to war.

The short version

Fifty years after the fall of Saigon, historians and leaders still disagree about what the Vietnam War meant. The orthodox school includes historians such as David Halberstam, Neil Sheehan and George Herring. They argue the war could never have been won. American leaders mistook a long nationalist revolution against colonial rule for a Cold War fight with the Soviet Union and China. No amount of firepower could make an unpopular, corrupt government in Saigon legitimate. The revisionist school includes the military writer Harry Summers and historians such as Mark Moyar and Guenter Lewy. They argue the war was a just cause that could have been won. In their view, civilian politicians kept too tight a grip on the military and banned ground attacks on North Vietnamese bases across the border. They also say General Creighton Abrams’ pacification effort was working before Congress cut off South Vietnam. The debate left behind the "Vietnam Syndrome." Americans and military leaders became unwilling to send troops abroad without strong public support, a clear way out and overwhelming force. The Weinberger-Powell Doctrine put those rules in writing.

What to know for the test

  1. Orthodox vs. revisionist. The main debate to know for AP essays and DBQs about U.S. foreign policy.
  2. The Vietnam Syndrome. After the war, the public and Congress did not want to back open-ended military action abroad.
  3. The Powell Doctrine. General Colin Powell’s rules for sending troops. Use overwhelming force, protect a vital national interest, and have a clear way out.

Key terms

Orthodox Historiographical School
Historians who argue that the U.S. was wrong to fight in Vietnam and could not have won, because foreign military power could not break Vietnamese nationalism and the revolution’s staying power.
Revisionist Historiographical School
Historians who argue the war was a just cause that could have been won. They say politicians held the military back, and that Creighton Abrams’ pacification effort deserved more support.
The Powell Doctrine
Rules set out by General Colin Powell. The U.S. should send combat troops only when a vital national interest is at stake, and then with overwhelming force, clear goals, public support and a plan for getting out.

What happened

The full story. The small numbers open the source behind each claim.

Unlike World War II, which established a broad consensus regarding moral clarity and strategic necessity, the Vietnam War produced deep intellectual and political fractures. These historiographical divisions do not represent simple political talking points; they reflect fundamentally different interpretations of primary documents, military capabilities, and political legitimacy.

The **Orthodox or Liberal Realist school** (David Halberstam, Arthur Schlesinger Jr., George Herring) views the war as an unwinnable quagmire from the start. In this interpretation, American leaders were blinded by rigid Cold War containment dogmatism, misreading a genuine post-colonial nationalist revolution as part of a monolithic Sino-Soviet conspiracy. Because South Vietnam’s government lacked indigenous political legitimacy, no quantity of American military power could construct a viable non-communist state.

The **Revisionist or Conservative school** (Guenter Lewy, Harry Summers, Lewis Sorley) argues that the war was a noble cause to defend a developing ally against totalitarian aggression, and was militarily winnable. Revisionists contend that General Creighton Abrams’ "One War" strategy successfully pacified the countryside after 1968, and that South Vietnam’s collapse in 1975 was caused not by military failure, but by congressional aid cuts, media bias, and political abandonment.

Over the past two decades, the opening of Vietnamese, Soviet, and Chinese archives has fueled a **Post-Revisionist and Internationalist school** (Fredrik Logevall, Lien-Hang Nguyen, Pierre Asselin). This scholarship moves beyond the winnable/unwinnable dichotomy by treating Vietnamese actors not as passive pawns, but as autonomous agents who manipulated their superpower sponsors. It reveals Hanoi as an ideologically militant regime led by Le Duan that suppressed domestic moderates, while restoring agency to South Vietnamese civil society. Concurrently, literary voices such as North Vietnamese veterans **Bao Ninh** (*The Sorrow of War*) and **Duong Thu Huong** challenged official heroic state memory, while the museum in Ho Chi Minh City evolved through three distinct name changes (*Exhibition House for US and Puppet Crimes* to *War Crimes Museum* to *War Remnants Museum*) reflecting diplomatic normalization in 1995.

Key dates

  1. 1972

    Halberstam publishes The Best and the Brightest

    A founding orthodox book about how smart, confident officials led the country into war.

  2. 1981

    Summers publishes On Strategy

    A revisionist book arguing the military should have focused on blocking invasion routes from the North.

  3. 1990

    Bao Ninh publishes The Sorrow of War

    A North Vietnamese veteran’s novel shows the war as grief and trauma, not the heroic story the state told.

  4. 1995

    The U.S. and Vietnam restore relations

    President Bill Clinton and Prime Minister Vo Van Kiet restore full relations between the two countries.

  5. 2012

    Lien-Hang Nguyen publishes Hanoi’s War

    A study based on Vietnamese records of Le Duan’s police state, his purges and his wartime diplomacy.

The goal

Derive military and foreign policy lessons from the conflict to guide modern national security doctrine.

What they assumed
  • Orthodox: Military power cannot substitute for local political legitimacy in counterinsurgency.
  • Revisionist: Political restrictions and civilian micromanagement prevented the military from achieving victory.
What they argued about
  • Whether Kennedy would have withdrawn had he lived past 1963.
  • Whether the "decent interval" was a deliberate Nixon strategy or an ex-post facto critique.

Actors: Official State Historians vs Independent Researchers & Diaspora Voices

The goal

Balance official party triumphalism ("The Great Patriotic Resistance Against American Imperialism") with archival evidence and diaspora accounts.

What they assumed
  • Official Party History: Victory was the inevitable triumph of Marxism-Leninism and national unity under the Communist Party.
  • Archival & Diaspora Scholarship: Explores the immense human sacrifice, internal political repression, and the distinct non-communist identity of South Vietnam.
What they argued about
  • Ongoing official sensitivity regarding controversial episodes: the 1968 Hue civilian massacres, the 1979 Sino-Vietnamese War, and re-education camp conditions.
