How historians argue about the war

Could the United States have won the Vietnam War?

Orthodox historians say no, and revisionists say yes. Their argument still shapes how Americans decide whether to send troops abroad.

What happened

Why historians still disagree

World War II left most Americans agreed on why it was fought and that it was right. Vietnam left them divided, and historians still disagree about it. Their arguments come from reading the same documents in different ways, from different views of what each army could do, and from different answers to one question: did the government in Saigon have its people’s support?

The Orthodox school

The Orthodox school, also called the liberal realist school, includes David Halberstam, Arthur Schlesinger Jr. and George Herring. These historians see the war as a quagmire that could never be won. In their view, American leaders were blinded by a rigid belief in containing communism. They mistook a real nationalist revolution against colonial rule for part of a single Soviet and Chinese plot. Because South Vietnam’s government never won the loyalty of its own people, no amount of American military power could build a lasting non-communist state.

The Revisionist school

The Revisionist school, also called the conservative school, includes Guenter Lewy, Harry Summers and Lewis Sorley. These historians argue that defending South Vietnam from communist attack was a noble cause, and that the war could have been won. They say General Creighton Abrams’ One War strategy had made the countryside largely secure after 1968. In their view, South Vietnam fell in 1975 because Congress cut aid, the press was biased and the United States walked away, and not because its army was beaten.

Since the 1990s

The Post-Revisionist school

In the past twenty years, archives in Vietnam, the former Soviet Union and China have opened. That has produced a Post-Revisionist or international school, including Fredrik Logevall, Lien-Hang Nguyen and Pierre Asselin. These historians move past the question of whether the war was winnable. They treat the Vietnamese on every side as people who made their own choices and played their big-power backers against each other. Their work shows Hanoi under Le Duan as a hard-line government that crushed moderates at home, and it gives South Vietnamese society back its own voice. Vietnamese writers have challenged the official heroic story too, among them the North Vietnamese veterans Bao Ninh, author of The Sorrow of War, and Duong Thu Huong. Even the war museum in Ho Chi Minh City has changed its name three times, from Exhibition House for US and Puppet Crimes to War Crimes Museum to War Remnants Museum, as relations with the United States returned to normal in 1995.

The short version

Fifty years after the fall of Saigon, historians and leaders still disagree about what the Vietnam War meant. The orthodox school includes historians such as David Halberstam, Neil Sheehan and George Herring. They argue the war could never have been won. American leaders mistook a long nationalist revolution against colonial rule for a Cold War fight with the Soviet Union and China. No amount of firepower could make an unpopular, corrupt government in Saigon legitimate. The revisionist school includes the military writer Harry Summers and historians such as Mark Moyar and Guenter Lewy. They argue the war was a just cause that could have been won. In their view, civilian politicians kept too tight a grip on the military and banned ground attacks on North Vietnamese bases across the border. They also say General Creighton Abrams’ pacification effort was working before Congress cut off South Vietnam. The debate left behind the Vietnam Syndrome. Americans and military leaders became unwilling to send troops abroad without strong public support, a clear way out and overwhelming force. The Weinberger-Powell Doctrine put those rules in writing.

Key dates

  1. 1972

    Halberstam publishes The Best and the Brightest

    A founding orthodox book about how smart, confident officials led the country into war.

  2. 1981

    Summers publishes On Strategy

    A revisionist book arguing the military should have focused on blocking invasion routes from the North.

  3. 1990

    Bao Ninh publishes The Sorrow of War

    A North Vietnamese veteran’s novel shows the war as grief and trauma, not the heroic story the state told.

  4. 1995

    The U.S. and Vietnam restore relations

    President Bill Clinton and Prime Minister Vo Van Kiet restore full relations between the two countries.

  5. 2012

    Lien-Hang Nguyen publishes Hanoi’s War

    A study based on Vietnamese records of Le Duan’s police state, his purges and his wartime diplomacy.

    Congress and later presidents, 1973–1991

    Congress passed the War Powers Act in 1973, and American leaders grew wary of long wars abroad.

  2. 2

    What they expected

    Limits on presidential power and a cautious public would keep the country out of another Vietnam.

    What they got wrong.One war’s lessons do not fit every war, and leaders argued for decades over what Vietnam had taught.

  3. 3

    What happened

    For almost twenty years, the United States avoided large ground wars abroad.

    The response.In 1984 Defense Secretary Caspar Weinberger set out conditions for using force, and Colin Powell later built on them.

  4. 4

    Where it led

    After the 1991 Gulf War, President George H.W. Bush declared that the country had put the Vietnam Syndrome behind it. The argument over Vietnam’s lessons came back with the wars after 9/11.

Learn from the war in ways that guide how the United States uses force today.

What they assumed

Hanoi

Who decided: Official State Historians vs Independent Researchers & Diaspora Voices

What they wanted

Square the party’s official story of a great patriotic victory with what the archives and the refugees have to say.

What they assumed

  • The party’s official history calls victory the certain result of Marxism-Leninism and national unity under the Communist Party.
  • Scholars working from archives and from refugee accounts stress the huge human cost, repression inside the North and South Vietnam’s separate, non-communist identity.

What they argued about

  • Some topics are still sensitive in Vietnam today, among them the killings of civilians at Hue in 1968, the 1979 war with China and conditions in the re-education camps.

On April 4, 1995, an Associated Press wire reported the Vietnamese government’s release of comprehensive casualty statistics for the 1954–1975 war, disclosing that 1.1 million North Vietnamese and Viet Cong combatants were killed and 600,000 wounded, alongside civilian casualty estimates of approximately 2 million dead across Vietnam.

Four ways historians explain the war

Each generation of historians brought new records and new questions. Pick a school to see what it argues, what it leans on, and where its critics push back.

Orthodox school

1960s to 1980s, the first view to take hold. Read: David Halberstam, Arthur Schlesinger Jr., George C. Herring

The war was a tragedy that could never have been won. American leaders were blinded by a rigid belief in containing communism and mistook a real revolution against colonial rule for part of a single Soviet and Chinese plot. Because South Vietnam’s government never won its own people’s loyalty, no amount of American military power could build a lasting non-communist state.

What it gets right
  • Correctly predicted that wearing down the enemy would not break Vietnamese nationalism.
  • Documented the corruption, religious persecution and weakness of the governments in Saigon.
  • Showed what fighting an undeclared war cost Americans at home, in law, in society and in trust.
What critics say it misses
  • Often treated the Vietnamese on both sides as background or as innocent farmers, not as people with their own political goals.
  • Underplayed how much Hanoi was a hard-line communist police state, not only a nationalist movement.
  • Often waved off South Vietnam as a puppet and ignored the non-communist nationalists who lived there.

The evidence it leans on: The Pentagon Papers, internal CIA warnings such as a 1966 memo, records of the dysfunction under Diem, and the protests and casualty lists at home.
