America takes over the war

Why couldn’t half a million American troops wear North Vietnam down?

Westmoreland measured success in enemy dead. The count kept climbing, and the war kept going.

What happened

February–March 1965

Johnson sends in the Marines

In February 1965, the Viet Cong attacked the U.S. base at Pleiku. President Johnson answered with Operation Rolling Thunder, a long bombing campaign against North Vietnam. The idea was called graduated pressure: raise the bombing step by step until Hanoi agreed to talk, without pulling China or the Soviet Union into the war. On March 8, 1965, two battalions of Marines landed at Da Nang. They were the first U.S. ground combat troops in Vietnam, and the American role changed from advising to fighting.

1965–1967

Counting bodies

General William Westmoreland’s plan was attrition: kill enemy soldiers faster than they could be replaced. His troops went on search-and-destroy missions backed by heavy firepower, and progress was measured in body counts. In November 1965, the 1st Cavalry Division fought North Vietnamese army regiments in the Ia Drang Valley. By U.S. body counts, the Americans killed more than ten enemy soldiers for every one they lost. Westmoreland took this as proof that he could reach the crossover point, when the enemy would lose men faster than it could replace them. But the communist side started about 75 to 80 percent of the firefights. That let them choose when to fight and when to slip back across the border to safety. About 200,000 young men came of draft age in North Vietnam every year, more than enough to replace the losses. In 1967, the U.S. command in Saigon quietly stopped publishing its estimates of how many enemy troops were coming south.

1965–1967

Meanwhile, in Hanoi

In Hanoi, a power struggle was going on that Washington barely saw. By the mid-1960s, Ho Chi Minh’s role was mostly ceremonial, and First Secretary Le Duan made the big decisions. General Vo Nguyen Giap and others wanted a slow guerrilla war. Le Duan wanted big offensives, and he won by arresting hundreds of moderates in what became known as the Anti-Party Affair. At home, all of North Vietnam was organized for war. Concrete one-person shelters lined Hanoi’s sidewalks, and city people were moved to the countryside. More than 230,000 Youth Volunteers, over half of them women, kept the Ho Chi Minh Trail open under constant bombing.

The short version

In March 1965, President Johnson sent the first American ground combat troops, 3,500 Marines, to Da Nang. He also launched Operation Rolling Thunder, a three-year bombing campaign against North Vietnam. General William Westmoreland fought a war of attrition. He used heavy firepower, B-52 strikes and helicopter-borne troops to try to kill enemy soldiers faster than North Vietnam could replace them. That was the crossover point. In November 1965, the Battle of the Ia Drang Valley showed that American helicopters and firepower could cause huge losses. It also taught North Vietnamese commanders to grab them by the belt buckle, fighting so close that American air power could not be used without killing Americans. A Pentagon study found that communist forces started more than 75% of firefights. They could control their own losses, which broke the logic of attrition.

How one decision played out

  1. 1

    The decision

    Johnson and Westmoreland; Le Duan in Hanoi, 1965–1967

    Johnson sent nearly half a million troops to fight a war of attrition. Le Duan readied North Vietnam for one huge offensive.

  2. 2

    What they expected

    Washington thought Hanoi would run out of soldiers. Hanoi thought a nationwide attack on the cities would set off an uprising in the South.

    What they got wrong.Washington counted bodies while the enemy chose when and where to fight. Hanoi overestimated how many southerners would rise up.

  3. 3

    What happened

    American firepower killed huge numbers of enemy soldiers, but men and supplies kept coming down the Ho Chi Minh Trail.

    The response.Hanoi finished its plans to attack South Vietnam’s cities all at once in early 1968.

  4. 4

    Where it led

    Johnson’s officials kept telling Americans the war was being won. When the attack came, much of the public stopped believing them.

What each side wantedWashington and Hanoi, judged by the same questions

Washington

Who decided: Lyndon B. Johnson, Robert S. McNamara, Gen. William Westmoreland, Gen. Earle Wheeler

What they wanted

Kill communist soldiers faster than Hanoi could replace them, so it would give up the war in the South, without drawing in China or the Soviet Union.

What they assumed

  • Firepower, helicopters and body counts could show whether America was winning against a country of farmers.

  • North Vietnam had a breaking point, and enough losses would push it to negotiate.

What they argued about

  • In 1965 Undersecretary of State George Ball warned that once American troops entered combat, the country would be stuck in a war it could not finish.

  • By mid-1967 Robert McNamara had privately decided that Rolling Thunder was not working and that attrition would cost too much.

Pressures at home

  • Rising anti-war sentiment, draft card burnings, and the March on the Pentagon in October 1967.

  • Johnson paid for both the Great Society and the war without raising taxes, and prices at home began to climb.

Hanoi

Who decided: Le Duan, General Vo Nguyen Giap, Nguyen Chi Thanh, Ho Chi Minh

What they wanted

Fight a long war, take the bombing, keep the trail open and outlast America’s patience.

What they assumed

  • American voters would not put up with a long, bloody war for a goal they did not understand.

  • By grabbing the enemy by the belt and fighting at close range, communist troops could keep American planes and artillery from firing.

What they argued about

  • General Nguyen Chi Thanh wanted big battles against American units. Giap wanted to save his forces and fight as guerrillas.

  • Le Duan’s side overruled the moderates, arrested officers who disagreed and began planning a nationwide offensive.

Pressures at home

  • Operation Rolling Thunder bombed the North, so Hanoi moved factories and hospitals into caves and the countryside, and families lived on rations.

U.S. government record, TOP SECRET (Declassified 2004)

CIA Intelligence Memorandum: The Strategy Debate in Hanoi

Directorate of Intelligence, Central Intelligence Agency, Office of Current Intelligence, CIA, July 18, 1966

Refutes the perception that Hanoi was monolithic; reveals the high-stakes debate between large-unit offensive warfare (which culminated in Tet) and protracted defensive conservation.

A summary in our words, not a quotation

The July 18, 1966 CIA intelligence memorandum reported evidence of tactical debate within Hanoi’s leadership between General Nguyen Chi Thanh (who advocated aggressive, large-unit conventional engagements to blunt American combat forces) and Defense Minister Vo Nguyen Giap (who urged force preservation, prolonged attrition, and decentralized guerrilla tactics).

Held by: CIA Freedom of Information Act Electronic Reading Room

Eyewitness reporting, Published Eyewitness Account

"Hanoi—1968" (Eyewitness Dispatch from North Vietnam)

Mary McCarthy, Author and Eyewitness Observer, May 23, 1968

Provides a rare, contemporaneous Western eyewitness record of how aerial bombardment operated in practice: rather than coercing capitulation, it stimulated decentralized civil defense and collective endurance.

A summary in our words, not a quotation

In her May 1968 eyewitness report from Hanoi, author Mary McCarthy described street-level life under American aerial bombardment, reporting individual precast concrete cylinder bomb shelters spaced along sidewalks and widespread evacuation of children and the elderly to rural villages, observing that bombardment reinforced civilian social mobilization and collective discipline rather than causing urban collapse.

Held by: The New York Review of Books, Vol. 10, No. 10

Three ways to read the numbers

  • Westmoreland’s view

    Enough firepower, from artillery to B-52 strikes, would make the war too costly for Hanoi to keep fighting.

  • The Pentagon analysts’ view

    The enemy chose when and where to fight in most battles, so it could slow down whenever its losses got too high.

  • Le Duan’s view

    Hanoi accepted losses of ten to one or worse, betting that American voters would tire of the war before North Vietnam ran out of soldiers.
