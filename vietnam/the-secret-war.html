Chapter 6, 1961–1972

The secret war

The Phoenix Program, herbicide spraying, and the hidden war in Laos

What did America’s hidden war in Laos and South Vietnam achieve, and what did it cost?

The war spread in secret into neutral countries next door. This chapter covers the CIA’s war in Laos and the disputed Phoenix Program.

The short version

To keep the war in the South going, Hanoi built the Truong Son supply network, known as the Ho Chi Minh Trail. It ran through the jungle mountains of Laos and Cambodia, both neutral countries. Over 16 years it grew from a footpath into 12,000 miles of roads, truck parks, underground fuel pipelines and repair depots. The U.S. wanted to cut it without openly breaking the 1962 Geneva Accords, which guaranteed Laos’s neutrality. So it fought a "Secret War." American planes flew more than 580,000 bombing missions and dropped more than 2 million tons of bombs on Laos. That equals one planeload every eight minutes for nine years. The CIA also raised a guerrilla army of 30,000 Hmong fighters under General Vang Pao. In South Vietnam, the CIA started the Phoenix Program. It "neutralized" more than 81,000 suspected members of the Viet Cong’s civilian network by identifying, interrogating and killing them.

What to know for the test

  1. The Ho Chi Minh Trail. The supply route that kept communist forces in the South armed and reinforced through years of American bombing.
  2. Laos, the most bombed nation. To cut the trail, the U.S. bombed neutral Laos on a massive scale and did not admit it. Tens of millions of unexploded cluster bomblets were left behind.
  3. The Phoenix Program (Phung Hoang). It targeted the Viet Cong’s civilian shadow government. Corruption, arbitrary arrests and targeted killings made it one of the most disputed programs of the war.

Key terms

Ho Chi Minh Trail (Truong Son Network)
A 12,000-mile web of hidden roads, jungle paths, river crossings and fuel pipelines through Laos and Cambodia. North Vietnam used it to move troops and supplies to South Vietnam.
Phoenix Program (Phung Hoang)
A joint CIA and South Vietnamese intelligence program (1967–1972). Its aim was to find and break up the Viet Cong’s civilian shadow government in southern villages.
The Secret War in Laos (1964–1973)
A hidden U.S. military and CIA campaign. It backed the Royal Lao Government and Hmong guerrillas against the communist Pathet Lao and North Vietnam’s supply routes.

What happened

The full story. The small numbers open the source behind each claim.

Recognizing that military sweeps failed to eliminate the Viet Cong’s civilian governance apparatus—which collected taxes, ran schools, and recruited fighters—the CIA and South Vietnamese security agencies developed the Phoenix Program (Phung Hoang) in 1967–1968. Directed under CORDS (Civil Operations and Revolutionary Development Support), Phoenix coordinated intelligence across military and police organs to identify and "neutralize" the Viet Cong Infrastructure (VCI). Official figures reported to Congress in 1971 indicated that 81,740 individuals were neutralized: 33,358 captured, 22,013 defected, and 26,369 killed. However, testimony revealed that roughly 87 percent of those killed died during regular military operations and artillery strikes rather than targeted police actions, as monthly quotas led to widespread padding of reports with ordinary combat casualties.

Between 1962 and 1971, the U.S. Air Force conducted Operation Ranch Hand, spraying an estimated 19 million gallons of tactical herbicides over rural South Vietnam to deprive guerrilla forces of vegetative cover and food crops. Roughly 11 million gallons consisted of Agent Orange, contaminated with the persistent carcinogen 2,3,7,8-TCDD dioxin. Over 5 million acres of forest and 500,000 acres of crops were defoliated. The chemical legacy persisted for decades, causing elevated rates of cancer, birth defects, and chronic illnesses among Vietnamese civilians and American veterans. In an underreported domestic aftermath, contaminated UC-123 aircraft were transferred to the U.S. Air Force Reserve and flown domestically on cargo missions from 1972 to 1982.

Concurrently, the CIA prosecuted a massive "Secret War" in neighboring Laos, organizing an army of roughly 30,000 ethnic Hmong under General Vang Pao to interdict the Ho Chi Minh Trail. Supported by Air America transport planes and intense covert B-52 strikes, Laos became the most heavily bombed country per capita in human history. While Phoenix and pacification efforts disrupted insurgent political networks, their heavy-handed implementation and civilian casualties alienated significant segments of the rural population.

Key dates

  1. Nov 1961

    Kennedy approves chemical spraying

    Diem asks for spraying from the air. Kennedy approves it one mission at a time as a test.

  2. Jan 1962

    Operation Ranch Hand begins

    Planes start spraying plant-killing chemicals over South Vietnam.

  3. May 1967

    CORDS is set up

    Robert Komer brings the civilian and military pacification efforts together under MACV.

  4. July 1968

    The Phoenix Program starts

    President Thieu orders Phung Hoang to find and break up the Viet Cong’s civilian network.

  5. July 1971

    Congress holds hearings on Phoenix

    William Colby and former intelligence soldiers tell the House about quotas and abuses.

  6. 1971–1972

    Ranch Hand ends

    Spraying stops after scientific studies on dioxin poisoning and protests at home.

Cause and effect

One decision from this chapter, followed step by step to a result nobody planned.

  1. 1

    The decision

    MACV, CIA, & South Vietnamese Authorities, 1962–1971

    Institutionalized the Phoenix Program to eliminate insurgent cadres and conducted Operation Ranch Hand to defoliate rural sanctuaries.

  2. 2

    What they missed

    They assumed.Dismantling the political infrastructure and stripping vegetative cover would break the insurgency’s hold on the rural population.

    The risk nobody checked.Quota-driven arrests and widespread chemical devastation generated substantial civilian alienation, reinforcing the grievances that fueled insurgency.

  3. 3

    What happened next

    First.Over 80,000 individuals were neutralized under Phoenix, and millions of forest acres were destroyed.

    The other side’s answer.Insurgent networks adapted by shifting base areas across borders and relying increasingly on conventional northern regular units.

  4. 4

    What it led to

    The programs damaged the moral standing of the allied effort internationally and produced an enduring ecological and health crisis spanning generations.

What Washington believed and what Hanoi believed

1961–1972
Who decided in WashingtonWhite House, Pentagon, CIA

What Washington wanted

Actors: Robert Komer, William Colby, Richard Helms, Ngo Dinh Diem

The goal

Eradicate the political shadow government of the Viet Cong and deny insurgents forest concealment and agricultural sustenance.

What they assumed
  • The insurgency was sustained primarily by a coercive political infrastructure that could be dismantled through centralized intelligence collection and police action.
  • Herbicides were an efficient, economical weapon to protect American patrol bases and convoys without long-term ecological consequences.
What they argued about
  • Controversy erupted in Congress and the media labeling Phoenix a "civilian assassination program," leading the CIA to defend it as an essential counterinsurgency program padded by conventional combat reporting.
  • State Department officials initially resisted herbicide defoliation in 1961, fearing international condemnation for chemical warfare.
Who decided in HanoiPolitburo, Party Central Committee

What Hanoi wanted

Actors: Le Duan, Central Office for South Vietnam (COSVN), Nguyen Van Linh

The goal

Protect underground party cadres, maintain rural political control, and counter pacification programs.

What they assumed
  • The rural population’s nationalist sentiment and land grievances would enable the Viet Cong infrastructure to regenerate despite government arrests.
  • American chemical defoliation would reinforce anti-imperialist propaganda and drive uncommitted peasants into the revolutionary camp.
What they argued about
  • COSVN recognized Phoenix as a severe operational threat that decimated southern party apparatus in many provinces between 1968 and 1971, forcing cadres to operate from remote base areas.
