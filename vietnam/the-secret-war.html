Chapter 6, 1961–1972

The Secret War

What did America’s secret war in Laos and South Vietnam cost?

The fighting spread into Laos, a neutral country, without most Americans knowing. Unexploded American bombs still lie in Laotian farmland.

What happened

1967–1971

The Phoenix Program

Army sweeps could not destroy the Viet Cong’s hidden government. It collected taxes, ran schools and recruited fighters. So in 1967–1968, the CIA and South Vietnamese security agencies created the Phoenix Program, Phung Hoang in Vietnamese. It was run by CORDS (Civil Operations and Revolutionary Development Support). Phoenix pooled what the military and police knew in order to find the members of this hidden network and remove them by capture, persuasion or killing. American figures for 1968 to 1972 counted 81,740 people removed: 33,358 captured, 22,013 who switched sides and 26,369 killed. But testimony showed that about 87 percent of those killed died in regular military operations and artillery fire, not in targeted police actions. Monthly quotas led officials to pad their reports with ordinary combat deaths.

1962–1971

Operation Ranch Hand

From 1962 to 1971, the U.S. Air Force ran Operation Ranch Hand. Its planes sprayed about 19 million gallons of plant-killing chemicals over the South Vietnamese countryside to strip away the jungle cover and crops the guerrillas relied on. About 11 million gallons were Agent Orange, which was contaminated with 2,3,7,8-TCDD, a dioxin that causes cancer and stays in the environment for years. More than 5 million acres of forest and 500,000 acres of crops were stripped bare. The harm lasted for decades, with higher rates of cancer, birth defects and long-term illness among Vietnamese civilians and American veterans. Some of the contaminated UC-123 planes were later handed to the Air Force Reserve, which flew them inside the United States from 1972 to 1982.

1961–1973

The war in Laos

At the same time, the CIA was running a large secret war in neighboring Laos. It organized an army of Hmong fighters under General Vang Pao. By the end of 1963, a reported 20,000 of them were armed, and they raided North Vietnamese supply depots and ambushed trucks. With Air America transport planes and heavy secret B-52 raids, Laos became the most heavily bombed country per person in history. Phoenix and the other pacification programs did damage the Viet Cong’s political network. But their rough methods and the civilians they killed turned many villagers against the government.

The short version

To keep the war in the South going, Hanoi built the Truong Son supply network, known as the Ho Chi Minh Trail. It ran through the jungle mountains of Laos and Cambodia, both neutral countries. Over 16 years it grew from a footpath into 12,000 miles of roads, truck parks, underground fuel pipelines and repair depots. The U.S. wanted to cut it without openly breaking the 1962 Geneva Accords, which guaranteed Laos’s neutrality. So it fought a Secret War. American planes flew more than 580,000 bombing missions and dropped more than 2 million tons of bombs on Laos. That equals one planeload every eight minutes for nine years. The CIA also raised a guerrilla army of thousands of Hmong fighters under General Vang Pao. In South Vietnam, the CIA started the Phoenix Program. It neutralized more than 81,000 suspected members of the Viet Cong’s civilian network by identifying, interrogating and killing them.

  1. Nov 1961

    Kennedy approves chemical spraying

  2. Jan 1962

    Operation Ranch Hand begins

    Planes start spraying plant-killing chemicals over South Vietnam.

  3. May 1967

    CORDS is set up

    Robert Komer brings the civilian and military pacification efforts together under MACV.

  4. July 1968

    The Phoenix Program starts

    President Thieu orders Phung Hoang to find and break up the Viet Cong’s civilian network.

  5. July 1971

    Congress holds hearings on Phoenix

    William Colby and former intelligence soldiers tell the House about quotas and abuses.

  6. 1971

    Ranch Hand ends

    Spraying stops after scientific studies on dioxin poisoning and protests at home.

What each side wantedWashington and Hanoi, judged by the same questions

Washington

Who decided: Robert Komer, William Colby, Richard Helms, Ngo Dinh Diem

What they wanted

Destroy the Viet Cong’s hidden government and take away the jungle cover and crops the guerrillas lived on.

What they assumed

  • The guerrillas depended on a network of officials who ruled by fear, and police work guided by good intelligence could take that network apart.

  • Herbicides were a cheap, efficient way to protect bases and convoys, with no lasting harm to the land.

What they argued about

  • Critics in Congress and the press called Phoenix a program for assassinating civilians. The CIA defended it and said ordinary combat deaths had padded its numbers.

  • In 1961 State Department officials pushed back against spraying herbicides, because they feared the world would call it chemical warfare.

Pressures at home

  • News of abuse during interrogations, the tiger cages on Con Son Island and chemical poisoning brought hard questions from the public and from Congress.

Hanoi

Who decided: Le Duan, Central Office for South Vietnam (COSVN), Nguyen Van Linh

What they wanted

Protect party members in the South, keep control of the villages and fight the pacification programs.

What they assumed

  • Villagers’ nationalism and anger over land would let the Viet Cong rebuild its network no matter how many people were arrested.

  • American spraying would push villagers who had not taken sides toward the revolution.

What they argued about

  • Communist headquarters in the South saw Phoenix as a serious threat. Between 1968 and 1971 it wrecked the party’s network in many provinces and forced officials to retreat to remote bases.

Pressures at home

  • The party lost many experienced organizers in the South, so Hanoi sent officials from the North to take their place.

CIA Intelligence Memorandum: The Strategy Debate in Hanoi

Directorate of Intelligence, Central Intelligence Agency, Office of Current Intelligence, CIA, July 18, 1966

Refutes the perception that Hanoi was monolithic; reveals the high-stakes debate between large-unit offensive warfare (which culminated in Tet) and protracted defensive conservation.

A summary in our words, not a quotation

The July 18, 1966 CIA intelligence memorandum reported evidence of tactical debate within Hanoi’s leadership between General Nguyen Chi Thanh (who advocated aggressive, large-unit conventional engagements to blunt American combat forces) and Defense Minister Vo Nguyen Giap (who urged force preservation, prolonged attrition, and decentralized guerrilla tactics).

Who actually did the killing?

87%

In regular combat

Most of the dead were soldiers or villagers killed in ordinary sweeps, artillery fire and ambushes. Province chiefs had monthly quotas from Saigon, so officials often listed these dead as Viet Cong officials after the fact.

13%

In targeted raids

The rest died in raids by police or by CIA-advised Provincial Reconnaissance Units. In 1971 William Colby told Congress that Phoenix was not an assassination program, but he admitted to abuses, torture and wrongful arrests by local police.
