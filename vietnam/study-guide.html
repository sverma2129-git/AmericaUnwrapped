Vietnam War study guide

Thirty years and six presidents, from Ho Chi Minh’s declaration of independence in 1945 to the fall of Saigon in 1975.

Built for U.S. History and AP U.S. History (Period 8). Everything here comes from the 12 chapters, so any answer can be traced back to where it is taught.

The presidents

Six presidents dealt with Vietnam, and Truman and Eisenhower share a tab here. Pick one to see what he decided and the mistake students make most often about those years.

19451975

Harry Truman and Dwight Eisenhower

Turning points from these years

Containment and the Domino Theory

What they did
Stopped seeing Ho Chi Minh as a nationalist and started seeing him as a tool of China and the Soviet Union. Paid 80% of France’s military costs ($2 billion). Turned down air strikes to save Dien Bien Phu (1954).
The turning point
The Geneva Accords (1954) split Vietnam at the 17th parallel. Eisenhower backed Ngo Dinh Diem in South Vietnam, and the 1956 elections to reunite the country were called off.
Mistake to avoid on the test
The Geneva line was not meant to be a permanent border. Article 6 said the 17th parallel was temporary until elections were held.

Essay practice

For AP U.S. History. Three practice prompts with sample outlines, and the Vietnam questions from past AP exams.

Practice prompts

Write your own thesis and list your evidence first. Then open the outline and compare.

  1. Prompt 1Analyze the ways in which the Vietnam War heightened social, political, and economic tensions in the United States. Focus your answer on the period 1964 to 1975.Tensions at home, 1964–1975Show outlineHide outline

    A sample thesis

    The war strained the country on all three fronts. Socially, the draft and televised combat split Americans by age and class and filled campuses with protest. Politically, the credibility gap and the Pentagon Papers destroyed trust in the president and pushed Congress to take back its war powers. Economically, paying for the war and the Great Society at the same time, without raising taxes, fed inflation.

    Evidence to use

    • The draft, whose burden fell hardest on working-class Americans
    • The Tet Offensive (1968) and Walter Cronkite’s “mired in stalemate” broadcast
    • The move into Cambodia and the Kent State shootings (1970)
    • The Pentagon Papers (1971) and New York Times Co. v. United States
    • Funding the war and the Great Society without raising taxes, which fed inflation
    • The War Powers Act, passed over Nixon’s veto in November 1973

    Mistakes to avoid

    • Covering only social tension and skipping the political and economic parts of the prompt
    • Listing protests without explaining what tension each one shows
    • Stopping in 1968 instead of following the prompt through 1975
  2. Prompt 2Analyze the international and domestic challenges the United States faced between 1968 and 1974, and evaluate how President Richard Nixon’s administration responded to them.Nixon’s challenges, 1968–1974Show outlineHide outline

    A sample thesis

    Nixon faced a war Americans no longer supported and a country divided over it. Abroad, he answered with Vietnamization, the Nixon Doctrine and wider bombing, and he used talks with Moscow and Beijing to press Hanoi. At home, he appealed to the Silent Majority, but the secret bombing of Cambodia, Kent State and the Pentagon Papers deepened distrust, and Congress answered by limiting his power.

    Evidence to use

    • Vietnamization: U.S. troops cut from 543,000 to about 24,000 by late 1972
    • The Nixon Doctrine (Guam, 1969)
    • Operation Menu and the 1970 move into Cambodia
    • The Kent State shootings and the Cooper-Church Amendment (1970)
    • The Christmas Bombing (1972) and the Paris Peace Accords (January 1973)
    • The War Powers Act (1973) and Nixon’s resignation over Watergate (1974)

    Mistakes to avoid

    • Answering only half the prompt: describe the challenges and also evaluate the responses
    • Treating Vietnamization as a quick end to the war: the U.S. withdrawal took four years
    • Leaving out the domestic side: protest, the Pentagon Papers and Congress pushing back
  3. Prompt 3Evaluate the extent to which the Vietnam War fostered change in the United States between 1960 and 1975.How much did the war change America? 1960–1975Show outlineHide outline

    A sample thesis

    The war changed how far Americans trusted their government and how much power Congress gave the president to make war. It changed far less about the goal of U.S. foreign policy: containment of communism guided every president from Kennedy to Ford.

    Evidence to use

    • The Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (1964) and the War Powers Act (1973)
    • The credibility gap after the Tet Offensive (1968)
    • The Pentagon Papers (1971) and the Supreme Court ruling on press freedom
    • Campus protest, the move into Cambodia and Kent State (1970)
    • Continuity: the Domino Theory and containment, from Kennedy to Nixon’s promises to Thieu

    Mistakes to avoid

    • Only listing changes: a prompt that asks to what extent wants you to weigh change against continuity
    • Ignoring the dates: the prompt starts in 1960, before American combat troops arrived
    • Describing events without saying what changed because of them

Glossary

Every key term in the unit, grouped by chapter. Search it when you just need a quick answer.

70 terms

Ch. 1Dien Bien Phu and the end of French rule6 terms
Ho Chi Minh

A Vietnamese nationalist and communist leader. He founded the Viet Minh in 1941 and declared the Democratic Republic of Vietnam in 1945.

›Why it matters and test tip

He joined the fight against colonial rule to communist organization, and led the movement that defeated France and then outlasted the United States.

On the test. He quoted the American Declaration of Independence in his 1945 independence speech.

Vo Nguyen Giap

A self-taught North Vietnamese general. He commanded Viet Minh forces, and later North Vietnam’s army, from 1946 into the 1970s.

›Why it matters and test tip

He planned the siege of Dien Bien Phu (1954) and helped direct the Tet Offensive (1968).

On the test. He was a leading voice for a long people’s war meant to wear down an enemy’s will to keep fighting.

17th Parallel

The temporary military line set by the 1954 Geneva Accords. It divided North and South Vietnam.

›Why it matters and test tip

It was meant to last two years as a ceasefire line. It became a fortified Cold War border when the 1956 elections were called off.

On the test. Linked to the Demilitarized Zone (DMZ) along the Ben Hai River.

Domino Theory

A Cold War idea made popular by President Eisenhower in 1954. It held that if one country fell to communism, its neighbors would fall too.

›Why it matters and test tip

Five presidents in a row used it to justify American involvement in Vietnam.

On the test. Shows up often. DBQs pair it with Eisenhower’s April 1954 press conference.

Battle of Dien Bien Phu (1954)

A 55-day siege in northwestern Vietnam. Viet Minh forces surrounded a French fortress in a valley and overran it.

›Why it matters and test tip

It ended France’s will to keep fighting the First Indochina War and led straight to the Geneva Accords.

On the test. Remember the trap: the French were in the valley, and Giap’s hidden guns were in the hills around them.

Geneva Accords of 1954

The international agreement that ended the First Indochina War. It divided Vietnam at the 17th parallel for the time being, with nationwide elections set for 1956.

›Why it matters and test tip

The division created North and South Vietnam. When the elections were called off, the temporary line became a Cold War border.

On the test. Article 6 said the 17th parallel was temporary and was not a permanent border.

Ch. 2Geneva splits Vietnam in two6 terms
Ngo Dinh Nhu

President Diem’s younger brother and closest adviser. He ran the secret police and the Strategic Hamlet Program.

›Why it matters and test tip

His crackdowns on Buddhist monks and on critics in 1963 set off the crisis that led Washington to accept a coup against Diem.

On the test. His wife, Madame Nhu, mocked the monks who burned themselves in protest, dismissing the deaths as barbecues.

Law 10/59

A 1959 decree from Diem that set up traveling military courts. They could execute anyone suspected of communist ties, sometimes by guillotine.

›Why it matters and test tip

It spread fear in the countryside. It also convinced former Viet Minh members that peaceful opposition was impossible, which pushed them toward armed struggle.

On the test. It helped lead Hanoi to approve armed struggle in the South in 1959 (Resolution 15).

SEATO

The Southeast Asia Treaty Organization. Secretary of State John Foster Dulles created this defense alliance in September 1954.

›Why it matters and test tip

The U.S. government used it as the legal basis for protecting South Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia.

On the test. The Gulf of Tonkin Resolution cites SEATO as a treaty duty that required the U.S. to act.

Ngo Dinh Diem

A strongly anti-communist Catholic politician. He was president of the Republic of Vietnam (South Vietnam) from 1955 until he was killed in a military coup in 1963.

›Why it matters and test tip

He was the leader America chose to build a non-communist South. His harsh rule and his reliance on his own family weakened his government.

On the test. Shows up often. He is central to questions about building South Vietnam and about the 1963 Buddhist Crisis.

National Liberation Front (NLF / Viet Cong)

The communist-led rebel organization formed in South Vietnam in December 1960. Its goals were to overthrow Diem’s government and reunite the country.

›Why it matters and test tip

It fought a guerrilla war in villages across South Vietnam, with heavy support from North Vietnam down the Ho Chi Minh Trail.

On the test. The name Viet Cong, short for Vietnamese Communist, was an insulting label from Diem’s government. Members called themselves the NLF.

Operation Passage to Freedom (1954–1955)

The move of about 900,000 refugees, most of them Catholic, from North Vietnam to the South after the Geneva partition. The U.S. Navy helped carry them.

›Why it matters and test tip

It gave Diem a loyal Catholic base of support in the South. It also raised tension with the Buddhist majority.

On the test. Many of these refugees became officials and army officers in Diem’s government.

Ch. 3Kennedy, Diem and the coup6 terms
Thich Quang Duc

A Buddhist monk who burned himself to death in Saigon on June 11, 1963. He was protesting the Diem government’s treatment of Buddhists.

›Why it matters and test tip

His death set off the Buddhist Crisis. It ended American public support for Ngo Dinh Diem and led to the coup of November 1963.

On the test. Buddhists preserved his heart, which survived the cremation, as a relic.

MACV

Military Assistance Command, Vietnam. It was the U.S. headquarters set up in Saigon in February 1962 to run all American military advising and fighting in the country.

›Why it matters and test tip

Its commanders were General Paul Harkins, then General William Westmoreland, then General Creighton Abrams. MACV ran U.S. military strategy in the war.

On the test. Kennedy created it in 1962 as the advisory effort outgrew the older command, called MAAG.

Agent Orange

A chemical that kills plants. It contained toxic dioxin. U.S. Air Force planes sprayed it in Operation Ranch Hand to strip away jungle cover and destroy enemy food crops.

›Why it matters and test tip

It caused serious health problems for decades, including birth defects and cancer, among Vietnamese civilians and American veterans.

On the test. Kennedy first approved spraying in late 1961.

Flexible Response

Kennedy’s defense policy. It replaced Eisenhower’s nuclear Massive Retaliation with more options: counterinsurgency, Special Forces (Green Berets), and regular forces matched to the size of the threat.

›Why it matters and test tip

It set the United States up to step into guerrilla wars like Vietnam a little at a time.

On the test. Exams often contrast it with Eisenhower’s nuclear brinkmanship.

Strategic Hamlet Program

A joint U.S. and South Vietnamese program that forced farmers to move into fortified villages. Barbed wire and sharpened bamboo stakes were meant to cut them off from the Viet Cong.

›Why it matters and test tip

Farmers resented being taken from their family land and graves. The Viet Cong got inside the hamlets anyway.

On the test. A classic case of a program that produced the opposite of what it was meant to do.

State Department Cable 243 (August 24, 1963)

A top-secret telegram drafted by Roger Hilsman and approved by Kennedy. It told Ambassador Henry Cabot Lodge Jr. that the U.S. would no longer accept Diem’s brother Nhu in power, and would support a coup if Diem refused to remove him.

›Why it matters and test tip

South Vietnamese generals who opposed Diem took it as a green light from Washington. The coup followed in November 1963.

On the test. It shows that the United States had a direct part in the overthrow of South Vietnam’s leader.

Ch. 4The Gulf of Tonkin4 terms
Gulf of Tonkin Resolution (1964)

A joint resolution of Congress. It authorized President Johnson to take all necessary measures to repel armed attack on U.S. forces and to prevent further aggression.

›Why it matters and test tip

It was the legal basis for raising U.S. involvement from 23,000 advisers to more than 540,000 combat troops, with no declaration of war.

On the test. A core AP U.S. History topic. It is often tested with the War Powers Act of 1973, which Congress passed to limit presidential war powers.

OPLAN 34A

A secret operation run by the CIA and the U.S. military command. South Vietnamese commandos in unmarked boats raided North Vietnam’s coast.

›Why it matters and test tip

Raids on the islands of Hon Me and Hon Ngu came just before North Vietnam attacked the nearby USS Maddox on August 2.

On the test. Key evidence that U.S. ships were not simply bystanders in international waters.

War Powers Act of 1973

A federal law passed over President Nixon’s veto. The president must tell Congress within 48 hours of sending troops into action, and must withdraw them after 60 days unless Congress approves.

›Why it matters and test tip

It was Congress’s answer to the Tonkin Resolution and the Pentagon Papers. The goal was to restore its check on the president’s power to make war.

On the test. A frequent DBQ and essay topic on presidential power and congressional oversight.

DeSoto Patrols

Spy missions by U.S. Navy destroyers off communist coasts. The ships recorded radar sites and radio signals.

›Why it matters and test tip

They put American warships close to North Vietnam’s waters while commando raids were under way.

On the test. Often compared to Cold War spy flights near Soviet borders.

Ch. 5America takes over the war6 terms
General William Westmoreland

A four-star U.S. Army general who commanded U.S. forces in Vietnam from 1964 to 1968. He backed search-and-destroy tactics, body counts and attrition.

›Why it matters and test tip

He assumed American technology and resources would force North Vietnam to give up. He was replaced after the 1968 Tet Offensive.

On the test. In November 1967, shortly before Tet, he told Americans he could see light at the end of the tunnel.

The Crossover Point

The point at which, by the U.S. command’s math, enemy losses would be greater than North Vietnam’s ability to replace troops through the draft and recruiting.

›Why it matters and test tip

It was the reasoning behind Westmoreland’s war of attrition. It failed because North Vietnam had far more people, and far more staying power, than the U.S. estimated.

On the test. A key term for explaining why the war’s measures of progress were flawed.

Free-Fire Zone

A rural area that civilians had been ordered to leave. American and South Vietnamese forces could then shell or bomb anyone moving there without asking for clearance.

›Why it matters and test tip

The result was heavy civilian casualties. Millions of farmers were driven into crowded refugee slums around Saigon and Da Nang.

On the test. It turned many rural people against the South Vietnamese government and the U.S. military.

Search and Destroy

The U.S. command’s main ground tactic. Helicopters dropped American infantry into hostile countryside to find enemy units, hit them with artillery and air strikes, and then leave.

›Why it matters and test tip

The troops did not stay to hold the ground. Communist forces moved back into villages as soon as the helicopters left, and farmers paid the price.

On the test. Contrast it with the enclave and clear-and-hold approaches.

Operation Rolling Thunder (1965–1968)

The long bombing campaign by the U.S. Air Force and Navy against North Vietnam. It was stepped up gradually and dropped more than 864,000 tons of bombs.

›Why it matters and test tip

It did not make Hanoi stop sending men and supplies south, and it did not wreck the North’s farm-based economy. It hardened North Vietnamese resolve and brought in Soviet air-defense aid.

On the test. It dropped more bomb tonnage on North Vietnam than fell on the whole Pacific theater in World War II.

Battle of the Ia Drang Valley (Nov 1965)

The first major battle between American soldiers (the 1st Cavalry Division) and North Vietnamese regular troops, fought in the Central Highlands.

›Why it matters and test tip

Both sides claimed victory. The U.S. decided its helicopters and firepower worked. The North Vietnamese learned to fight at close range.

On the test. The subject of the memoir and film We Were Soldiers Once... and Young.

Ch. 6The secret war6 terms
General Vang Pao

A major general in the Royal Lao Army and the military leader of the Hmong. He commanded the CIA-funded Secret Army in Laos.

›Why it matters and test tip

He led Hmong forces against the North Vietnamese for more than ten years. After the communists took power in 1975, he helped tens of thousands of Hmong refugees resettle in the United States.

On the test. A central figure in the history of Hmong Americans, especially in Minnesota and California.

Cluster Bombs (UXO)

Bombs dropped over Laos and Vietnam that scatter hundreds of baseball-sized bomblets, each meant to explode when it hits.

›Why it matters and test tip

Up to 30% did not explode. An estimated 80 million live bomblets were left in Laotian soil, and they have killed or injured more than 20,000 civilians since 1973.

On the test. A major topic in postwar bomb-clearing and reconciliation work.

William Colby

A senior CIA official who ran the civilian pacification effort (CORDS) and the Phoenix Program in South Vietnam. He later became Director of Central Intelligence.

›Why it matters and test tip

He defended the Phoenix Program before Congress in 1971. He admitted to abuses and insisted it was the most effective counterinsurgency program of the war.

On the test. A key figure in the Church Committee’s investigations of CIA secret operations.

Ho Chi Minh Trail (Truong Son Network)

A 12,000-mile web of hidden roads, jungle paths, river crossings and fuel pipelines through Laos and Cambodia. North Vietnam used it to move troops and supplies to South Vietnam.

›Why it matters and test tip

It was the supply line Hanoi’s war depended on. American planes bombed it again and again and never closed it for good.

On the test. It ran through neutral countries, which is why the U.S. fought secret, undeclared air wars there.

Phoenix Program (Phung Hoang)

A joint CIA and South Vietnamese intelligence program (1967–1972). Its aim was to find and break up the Viet Cong’s civilian shadow government in southern villages.

›Why it matters and test tip

It neutralized more than 81,000 suspects and killed about 26,000 of them. It badly damaged guerrilla networks in the countryside, and Congress condemned it for torture and killings without trial.

On the test. Often cited in questions about secret warfare, ethics and counterinsurgency.

The Secret War in Laos (1964–1973)

A hidden U.S. military and CIA campaign. It backed the Royal Lao Government and Hmong guerrillas against the communist Pathet Lao and North Vietnam’s supply routes.

›Why it matters and test tip

It was fought in secret, without approval from Congress, and made Laos the most heavily bombed country per person on Earth.

On the test. More than 80 million unexploded cluster bomblets, called bombies, still lie in Laotian farmland.

Ch. 7The Tet Offensive6 terms
The Wise Men

An informal group of senior former officials, including Dean Acheson, McGeorge Bundy and Omar Bradley. Johnson called them together in March 1968 to review the war.

›Why it matters and test tip

They shocked Johnson. Most of them told him the war could not be won by force and that the U.S. should begin to pull back.

On the test. It shows that the foreign-policy establishment no longer agreed on the war.

Battle of Khe Sanh

A 77-day siege from January to April 1968. Two North Vietnamese divisions surrounded 6,000 U.S. Marines at an isolated base near the DMZ and the border with Laos.

›Why it matters and test tip

President Johnson feared an American Dien Bien Phu and watched it closely. The siege pulled American air power away from the coastal cities just before Tet.

On the test. Heavy bombing, called Operation Niagara, held the base. The U.S. quietly abandoned it months later.

Eddie Adams Photograph

An Associated Press photograph taken on a Saigon street on February 1, 1968. It shows South Vietnam’s national police chief, Nguyen Ngoc Loan, shooting a captured Viet Cong officer, Nguyen Van Lem.

›Why it matters and test tip

It won the Pulitzer Prize and became one of the best-known antiwar images in the world. For many people it stood for the war’s brutality.

On the test. Exams often pair it with Nick Ut’s photograph known as Napalm Girl.

Tet Offensive (January–February 1968)

A huge surprise offensive by North Vietnamese and Viet Cong forces during the lunar new year. They attacked more than 100 cities, towns and bases across South Vietnam at once.

›Why it matters and test tip

It was the war’s turning point in American opinion. It convinced many leaders and much of the public that the war could not be won at a cost they would accept.

On the test. Shows up often. A key turning point in AP U.S. History Period 8.

The Credibility Gap

The public’s growing doubt that President Johnson and the military were telling the truth about progress in Vietnam.

›Why it matters and test tip

It wore down trust in the federal government and fed both antiwar protest and investigative reporting.

On the test. Television coverage of the Tet Offensive widened it, and the Pentagon Papers widened it again.

Walter Cronkite’s Stalemate Broadcast (Feb 27, 1968)

A special CBS News report. After a trip to Vietnam, the country’s most trusted news anchor gave his own view that the war was “mired in stalemate.”

›Why it matters and test tip

It put into words what many middle-class Americans had come to feel. It also helped convince President Johnson that he had lost support at home.

On the test. DBQs often use it as evidence that mainstream news coverage had shifted.

Ch. 8Nixon and Vietnamization6 terms
The Nixon Doctrine

A policy President Nixon announced on Guam in 1969. The U.S. would give Asian allies money and military aid, but the allies would have to supply their own ground troops.

›Why it matters and test tip

It moved American policy away from sending its own armies into wars in poorer countries, and toward backing allies and using air power.

On the test. It is the idea behind Vietnamization.

The Madman Theory

Nixon’s negotiating tactic. He wanted communist leaders to believe he was unpredictable and might use nuclear weapons if the war went on.

›Why it matters and test tip

He used it to press Hanoi, Moscow and Beijing to give ground in the Paris talks.

On the test. It is described in the diaries and memoirs of his chief of staff, H.R. Haldeman.

Operation Linebacker I (1972)

A massive U.S. bombing campaign, along with the mining of Haiphong harbor. Nixon ordered it to stop North Vietnam’s Easter Offensive.

›Why it matters and test tip

It was the first large-scale use of laser-guided smart bombs. They destroyed key rail lines and bridges and stopped Hanoi’s tanks in the South.

On the test. It showed that South Vietnam could survive an invasion only with overwhelming American air power.

Vietnamization

President Nixon’s policy of training and equipping South Vietnam’s forces to take over the fighting while American ground troops were withdrawn step by step.

›Why it matters and test tip

It cut U.S. troop levels from 543,000 in 1969 to under 25,000 by 1972. Draft calls and American casualties fell, while bombing increased.

On the test. Shows up often. AP exams pair it with the Nixon Doctrine.

Kent State Shootings (May 4, 1970)

Ohio National Guardsmen shot unarmed students at Kent State University during protests against the move into Cambodia. Four students were killed and nine were wounded.

›Why it matters and test tip

It set off a nationwide student strike that closed more than 400 colleges and universities. It divided the country and deepened opposition to the war.

On the test. A symbol of how divided the country was. Crosby, Stills, Nash & Young wrote the song Ohio about it.

Operation Menu (1969–1970)

The secret B-52 bombing of North Vietnamese bases inside neutral Cambodia. Its phases were code-named Breakfast, Lunch, Snack, Dinner and Dessert.

›Why it matters and test tip

Falsified flight records hid it from Congress and the public. It unsettled rural Cambodia and, some historians argue, helped the Khmer Rouge rise.

On the test. A proposed article of impeachment against Nixon in 1974 was based on it.

Ch. 9The Paris Peace Accords6 terms
Le Duc Tho

A senior member of North Vietnam’s Politburo. He led the secret talks with Henry Kissinger in Paris from 1969 to 1973.

›Why it matters and test tip

He and Kissinger were awarded the 1973 Nobel Peace Prize. He turned it down, saying there was still no real peace in Vietnam.

On the test. He shaped North Vietnam’s plan for the negotiations.

Operation Homecoming (1973)

The return of 591 American prisoners of war held by North Vietnam and the Viet Cong, between February and April 1973.

›Why it matters and test tip

It was the most emotional moment of the withdrawal for Americans. Pilots such as John McCain and James Stockdale came home.

On the test. It was shown live on national television and marked the public end of America’s fighting role.

The Palace File

A collection of 27 private letters from Presidents Nixon and Ford to President Thieu. They promised American military action if North Vietnam broke the 1973 Accords.

›Why it matters and test tip

Nguyen Tien Hung made them public in 1986. They showed that Thieu signed only because of promises that Congress later made impossible to keep.

On the test. It shows the gap between what a president can promise and what Congress will allow.

Paris Peace Accords (January 27, 1973)

The Agreement on Ending the War and Restoring Peace in Viet-Nam. The United States, North Vietnam, South Vietnam and the Viet Cong all signed it.

›Why it matters and test tip

It ended direct U.S. military involvement and freed 591 American prisoners of war (Operation Homecoming). It also let communist forces stay in the South.

On the test. Shows up often. It ended America’s part in the war, not the war between Vietnamese.

Operation Linebacker II (Christmas Bombing)

An 11-day bombing campaign by U.S. B-52s and fighter-bombers against Hanoi and Haiphong, from December 18 to 29, 1972.

›Why it matters and test tip

It was the heaviest bombing of the war. The U.S. lost 15 B-52s, and countries around the world condemned the raids before peace talks started again.

On the test. It did not change the main terms already agreed in October 1972.

Case-Church Amendment (June 1973)

A law passed by Congress that banned all U.S. military action, including bombing, in Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia after August 15, 1973.

›Why it matters and test tip

It passed by margins large enough to beat a veto. It took away the president’s power to keep Nixon’s secret promises of air support to South Vietnam.

On the test. It ended American military action in Indochina by law.

Ch. 10The fall of Saigon6 terms
General Van Tien Dung

A senior North Vietnamese general and chief of staff of the army. He planned and commanded the final 1975 offensive.

›Why it matters and test tip

His plan broke South Vietnam’s defenses in 55 days. He took over from General Giap as North Vietnam’s main battlefield commander.

On the test. He wrote North Vietnam’s official account of the campaign, Our Great Spring Victory.

The Column of Tears

The chaotic retreat of 200,000 ARVN soldiers and more than 400,000 civilian refugees down the dirt road Route 7B, from the Central Highlands to the coast in March 1975.

›Why it matters and test tip

North Vietnamese artillery shelled the column again and again, and only a small part of it reached the coast. It showed South Vietnam’s command falling apart.

On the test. One of the worst military and humanitarian disasters in Vietnam’s history.

President Duong Van Minh (Big Minh)

A South Vietnamese general who led the 1963 coup against Diem. He was South Vietnam’s last president, for just 48 hours in April 1975.

›Why it matters and test tip

On April 30, 1975, he ordered all ARVN soldiers over Saigon radio to stop fighting. The surrender spared the capital from street-by-street destruction.

On the test. He told the arriving communist officers he was ready to hand over power. They answered that he had no power left to give.

The Fall of Saigon (April 30, 1975)

North Vietnam’s army captured Saigon, the capital of South Vietnam. It ended the 55-day campaign that Hanoi called the Great Spring Victory.

›Why it matters and test tip

It ended the Vietnam War and the Republic of Vietnam. The country was united under the communist government in Hanoi, and Saigon was renamed Ho Chi Minh City.

On the test. Shows up often. It is the last event in the Vietnam timeline on most exams.

Operation Frequent Wind (April 29–30, 1975)

The emergency helicopter evacuation of more than 1,300 Americans and 5,500 South Vietnamese at risk. Helicopters flew them from the U.S. Embassy and Tan Son Nhut Air Base to aircraft carriers offshore.

›Why it matters and test tip

Its chaotic scenes became the last images of the American defeat. Hubert van Es photographed people climbing a ladder to a helicopter on a Saigon rooftop.

On the test. It stands for the rushed, painful end of America’s longest war of the 20th century.

Battle of Xuan Loc (April 9–21, 1975)

The last major stand by South Vietnam’s army. The ARVN 18th Division, under Brigadier General Le Minh Dao, defended a key highway town 40 miles northeast of Saigon.

›Why it matters and test tip

It goes against the common idea that South Vietnamese troops would not fight. The 18th Division held off three North Vietnamese divisions for nearly two weeks before it was surrounded.

On the test. The last stand of South Vietnam’s army.

Ch. 11After the war6 terms
Vietnam Veterans Memorial

A national memorial in Washington, D.C., dedicated in November 1982. Maya Lin, a 21-year-old architecture student at Yale, designed it.

›Why it matters and test tip

Its black granite wall, shaped like a V, lists more than 58,000 dead service members in the order they died. It changed how the country mourned and helped veterans and families heal.

On the test. It is the most visited memorial on the National Mall. It uses names for quiet reflection instead of heroic statues.

Maya Lin

An American architect and artist. As a college student, she won the 1981 national contest to design the Vietnam Veterans Memorial, which was judged without names.

›Why it matters and test tip

Many conservatives attacked her plain, nonpolitical design at first. It is now widely seen as one of the great American memorials.

On the test. She received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2016.

Doi Moi (Renovation)

Economic reforms that Vietnam’s Communist Party began in 1986. They moved the country from a Soviet-style planned economy to what it called a socialist-oriented market economy.

›Why it matters and test tip

Private businesses were allowed and foreign investment was welcomed. Vietnam became one of the fastest-growing economies in Southeast Asia.

On the test. Similar to China’s economic reforms under Deng Xiaoping.

The Vietnamese Boat People

More than 1.5 million Vietnamese refugees who fled communist rule, re-education camps and poverty after the war. They crossed the sea in crowded, unsafe boats between 1975 and the 1990s.

›Why it matters and test tip

Hundreds of thousands died at sea. Survivors built Vietnamese communities around the world, such as Little Saigon in California.

On the test. Shows up often in questions about modern immigration and human rights.

Re-Education Camps (Hoc Tap Cai Tao)

Forced-labor prison camps in the countryside, set up by the communist government after 1975. About 1 million South Vietnamese officers, government workers and intellectuals were held there.

›Why it matters and test tip

Their purpose was to crush political opposition and teach communist ideas. Tens of thousands died from hunger, untreated disease and hard labor.

On the test. They were a main reason so many people fled Vietnam.

U.S.–Vietnam Normalization (July 1995)

President Bill Clinton’s announcement that the United States and the Socialist Republic of Vietnam would restore full diplomatic and economic relations.

›Why it matters and test tip

It ended twenty years of trade bans and hostility. Trade between the two countries grew, and so did cooperation on security.

On the test. Two Vietnam veterans in the Senate led the way: John McCain, a former POW, and John Kerry.

Ch. 12How historians argue about the war6 terms
The Vietnam Syndrome

Americans’ unwillingness, after the defeat in Vietnam, to send U.S. forces into wars abroad.

›Why it matters and test tip

It held back American military action through the late 1970s and 1980s, until the 1991 Persian Gulf War.

On the test. Shows up often. Exams pair it with Ronald Reagan calling Vietnam a noble cause.

The Pentagon Papers (1971)

A secret 7,000-page Defense Department history of the war. Military analyst Daniel Ellsberg leaked it to the New York Times.

›Why it matters and test tip

It showed that four presidents’ administrations had misled the public and Congress about the war’s goals and progress. In New York Times Co. v. United States, the Supreme Court let the papers be published.

On the test. A key case on freedom of the press and holding presidents to account.

The Weinberger Doctrine (1984)

Six tests that Defense Secretary Caspar Weinberger said must be met before sending U.S. troops into combat abroad. They included a vital interest, a plan to win, and support from the public and Congress.

›Why it matters and test tip

It led directly to the Powell Doctrine. It reflected the military’s resolve never again to slide into a war step by step, as in Vietnam.

On the test. It sums up what U.S. military leaders agreed on after Vietnam.

Orthodox Historiographical School

Historians who argue that the U.S. was wrong to fight in Vietnam and could not have won, because foreign military power could not break Vietnamese nationalism and the revolution’s staying power.

›Why it matters and test tip

It is the most common view among university historians. It stresses that the U.S. backed a corrupt government in the South that most Vietnamese did not see as their own.

On the test. Key historians: George Herring, David Halberstam, Neil Sheehan, Stanley Karnow.

Revisionist Historiographical School

Historians who argue the war was a just cause that could have been won. They say politicians held the military back, and that Creighton Abrams’ pacification effort deserved more support.

›Why it matters and test tip

It is popular at military colleges and among conservatives. It argues that aid cuts by Congress and negative news coverage threw away a war that could have been won.

On the test. Key scholars: Harry Summers (On Strategy), Mark Moyar (Triumph Forsaken), Guenter Lewy.

The Powell Doctrine

Rules set out by General Colin Powell. The U.S. should send combat troops only when a vital national interest is at stake, and then with overwhelming force, clear goals, public support and a plan for getting out.

›Why it matters and test tip

It turned the lessons of Vietnam into military rules. The U.S. followed it in the 1991 Persian Gulf War.

On the test. Shows up often in questions about foreign policy after the Cold War.
