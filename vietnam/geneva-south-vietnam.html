Chapter 2, 1954–1960

Geneva splits Vietnam in two

The 17th parallel, Ngo Dinh Diem, and the start of the war in the South

Why were the elections promised at Geneva never held, and how did the war in the South begin?

This is how a temporary ceasefire line became two hostile countries, and how opposition in the South grew into the National Liberation Front (the Viet Cong).

The short version

The 1954 Geneva Accords called for nationwide elections in July 1956 to reunite Vietnam. But neither the United States nor the new State of Vietnam signed the final statement. Prime Minister Ngo Dinh Diem had American money and help from CIA officer Edward Lansdale. He removed Emperor Bao Dai in a rigged vote and declared the Republic of Vietnam. He refused to hold the elections because he expected Ho Chi Minh to win easily. Diem’s land rules, his favoring of Catholics, and his harsh "Denounce the Communists" campaigns turned villages against him. Armed resistance grew in the South and organized as the National Liberation Front (the Viet Cong) in December 1960.

What to know for the test

  1. No elections in 1956. U.S. and South Vietnamese officials blocked the vote because they expected Ho Chi Minh to win as much as 80% of it.
  2. Diem loses the countryside. He reversed the Viet Minh’s land handouts and replaced elected village chiefs with Catholic loyalists. Farmers resented both.
  3. The Viet Cong forms. The National Liberation Front (NLF) was created in December 1960. It was a southern coalition, armed and reinforced from the North.

Key terms

Ngo Dinh Diem
A strongly anti-communist Catholic politician. He was president of the Republic of Vietnam (South Vietnam) from 1955 until he was killed in a military coup in 1963.
National Liberation Front (NLF / Viet Cong)
The communist-led rebel organization formed in South Vietnam in December 1960. Its goals were to overthrow Diem’s government and reunite the country.
Operation Passage to Freedom (1954–1955)
The move of about 900,000 refugees, most of them Catholic, from North Vietnam to the South after the Geneva partition. The U.S. Navy helped carry them.

What happened

The full story. The small numbers open the source behind each claim.

The Geneva Conference concluded in July 1954 with an armistice dividing Vietnam temporarily at the 17th parallel. A 300-day open-border period produced "Operation Passage to Freedom," during which roughly 890,000 northern Vietnamese—predominantly Catholics, civil servants, and anti-communist families—migrated south with U.S. Navy transport, while approximately 100,000 southern Viet Minh fighters moved north for regroupment.

In Saigon, Ngo Dinh Diem, an austere Catholic nationalist, became prime minister under Emperor Bao Dai. With American financial and advisory backing, Diem defeated the Binh Xuyen syndicate and religious sects, staged a rigged 1955 referendum to depose Bao Dai, and proclaimed the Republic of Vietnam (RVN). When the July 1956 deadline for nationwide reunification elections arrived, Diem refused to consult with Hanoi, asserting that genuine democratic voting was impossible in the communist North. President Eisenhower acknowledged in his memoirs that had nationwide elections occurred, Ho Chi Minh would have likely received 80 percent of the vote.

Crucially, the permanent division was not caused by Diem’s refusal alone: Geneva was an international compromise where neither superpower was committed to enforcing unification; Hanoi was internally consumed by post-war economic restoration and the turbulent 1953–56 land reform campaign; and no neutral mechanism existed to oversee a nationwide vote. In the South, Diem’s regime reversed Viet Minh land reforms, returning land to traditional landlords and fueling intense rural anger, while his "Denounce the Communists" campaign decimated southern cadre networks. In January 1959, Hanoi passed Resolution 15, officially authorizing armed revolutionary struggle, which culminated in the creation of the National Liberation Front (NLF / "Viet Cong") in December 1960.

Key dates

  1. July 21, 1954

    The Geneva Accords

    A ceasefire is signed. Vietnam is split at the 17th parallel for now, with elections due in 1956.

  2. 1954–1955

    Operation Passage to Freedom

    Nearly 900,000 refugees move from North to South. About 100,000 southern fighters move North.

  3. Oct 26, 1955

    Diem declares the Republic of Vietnam

    Ngo Dinh Diem becomes president after a rigged vote removes Bao Dai.

  4. July 1956

    The 1956 elections never happen

    The vote is never held. The temporary line hardens into a Cold War border.

  5. Jan 1959

    Hanoi approves armed struggle

    The Party approves armed revolution in the South. Group 559 starts building the Ho Chi Minh Trail.

  6. Dec 20, 1960

    The National Liberation Front forms

    Rebel groups in the South unite as the NLF in Tay Ninh province.

Cause and effect

One decision from this chapter, followed step by step to a result nobody planned.

  1. 1

    The decision

    Diem Administration & Washington / Hanoi Politburo, 1955–1959

    Diem and Washington bypassed the 1956 elections to build an anti-communist state, while Hanoi authorized armed struggle in 1959 to preserve southern insurgent networks.

  2. 2

    What they missed

    They assumed.Washington assumed economic and police assistance could build a durable southern state; Hanoi assumed southern guerrilla warfare could topple Diem without triggering direct U.S. combat intervention.

    The risk nobody checked.Diem’s land reversal and religious favoritism generated deep rural resentment, while Washington and Hanoi both underestimated each other’s willingness to escalate.

  3. 3

    What happened next

    First.Rural assassinations of Saigon village officials increased, security deteriorated, and the NLF formed in December 1960.

    The other side’s answer.Incoming President John F. Kennedy dramatically surged military advisors, special forces, and helicopter units to South Vietnam.

  4. 4

    What it led to

    An unresolved post-colonial political struggle transformed into a militarized insurgency and counterinsurgency contest.

The question this leaves: How would the Kennedy administration respond to an insurgency that modern military technology seemed unable to contain?

What Washington believed and what Hanoi believed

1954–1960
Who decided in WashingtonWhite House, Pentagon, CIA

What Washington wanted

Actors: Dwight D. Eisenhower, John Foster Dulles, Edward Lansdale, Ngo Dinh Diem

The goal

Construct a stable, sovereign, non-communist nation-state in South Vietnam through nation-building and economic assistance, avoiding direct U.S. ground combat.

What they assumed
  • American financial aid and military training via MAAG (Military Assistance Advisory Group) could turn South Vietnam into a self-sustaining regional bulwark.
  • A divided Vietnam was preferable to a unified nation under Ho Chi Minh, which Washington viewed as a net loss in the global Cold War.
  • Ngo Dinh Diem was the only viable anti-communist, anti-French nationalist capable of consolidating authority in Saigon.
What they argued about
  • Ambassador J. Lawton Collins warned in 1955 that Diem’s nepotism and narrow political base would cause long-term instability, advising Washington to seek an alternative.
  • CIA operative Edward Lansdale and Secretary of State Dulles persuaded Eisenhower to override Collins, committing the administration firmly to Diem.
Who decided in HanoiPolitburo, Party Central Committee

What Hanoi wanted

Actors: Le Duan, Ho Chi Minh, Truong Chinh, Pham Van Dong

The goal

Consolidate the northern socialist base, heal war devastation, and pursue national reunification.

What they assumed
  • Hanoi initially anticipated that the 1954 Geneva Accords and diplomatic pressure would result in nationwide elections in 1956.
  • Soviet and Chinese patrons urged moderation and peaceful political struggle, seeking to avoid provoking direct American military intervention.
What they argued about
  • North-First vs. South-First: Truong Chinh prioritized northern economic reconstruction and land reform; Le Duan, having led the southern resistance, argued that inaction while Diem hunted southern cadres was fatal.
  • Le Duan’s faction successfully pushed through Resolution 15 in 1959, authorizing armed struggle and the reactivation of southern networks.
