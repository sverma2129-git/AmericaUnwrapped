Chapter 2, 1954–1960

Geneva splits Vietnam in two

Why were the 1956 elections never held?

Without a vote, the line at the 17th parallel hardened into a border. By 1960 Diem’s enemies in the South had formed the National Liberation Front, which Americans called the Viet Cong.

What happened

1954–1955

Vietnam splits, and nearly a million people move south

The Geneva Conference ended in July 1954 with a ceasefire that split Vietnam at the 17th parallel. The split was supposed to be temporary. For 300 days, people could cross the line freely. With help from U.S. Navy ships, about 890,000 people moved south in Operation Passage to Freedom. Most were Catholics, government workers and families who opposed the communists. About 100,000 Viet Minh fighters from the South went north.

1955–1956

Diem takes power and skips the vote

In Saigon, Ngo Dinh Diem became prime minister under Emperor Bao Dai. Diem was a strict Catholic and a nationalist. With American money and advisers, he defeated the Binh Xuyen crime syndicate and the armed religious sects. In 1955 he held a rigged vote that removed Bao Dai, and he declared the Republic of Vietnam. Geneva had set July 1956 as the deadline for elections across the whole country. When the deadline came, Diem refused to talk with Hanoi. He said a fair vote was impossible in the communist North. President Eisenhower later wrote in his memoirs that Ho Chi Minh would probably have won about 80 percent of the vote.

1956–1960

The South turns against Diem

Diem’s refusal was not the only reason the split became permanent. Geneva was a compromise among many countries, and neither the United States nor the Soviet Union was willing to enforce a vote. Hanoi was busy rebuilding its economy and carrying out a harsh land reform campaign from 1953 to 1956. No neutral body existed to run a nationwide election. In the South, Diem gave land that the Viet Minh had handed out back to the old landlords, and many villagers were furious. His campaign against communists wiped out much of their network in the South. In January 1959, Hanoi’s leaders passed Resolution 15, which approved armed struggle in the South. In December 1960, they created the National Liberation Front, which Americans called the Viet Cong.

The short version

The 1954 Geneva Accords called for nationwide elections in July 1956 to reunite Vietnam. But neither the United States nor the new State of Vietnam signed the final statement. Prime Minister Ngo Dinh Diem had American money and help from CIA officer Edward Lansdale. He removed Emperor Bao Dai in a rigged vote and declared the Republic of Vietnam. He refused to hold the elections because he expected Ho Chi Minh to win easily. Diem’s land rules, his favoring of Catholics, and his harsh Denounce the Communists campaigns turned villages against him. Armed resistance grew in the South and organized as the National Liberation Front (the Viet Cong) in December 1960.

Key dates

    The Geneva Accords

  2. 1954–1955

    Operation Passage to Freedom

    Nearly 900,000 refugees move from North to South. About 100,000 southern fighters move North.

  3. Oct 26, 1955

    Diem declares the Republic of Vietnam

    Ngo Dinh Diem becomes president after a rigged vote removes Bao Dai.

    The 1956 elections never happen

    The vote is never held. The temporary line hardens into a Cold War border.

    Hanoi approves armed struggle

    The Party approves armed revolution in the South. Group 559 starts building the Ho Chi Minh Trail.

  6. Dec 20, 1960

    The National Liberation Front forms

    Rebel groups in the South unite as the NLF in Tay Ninh province.

    The decision

    Ngo Dinh Diem and Washington; Hanoi’s leaders, 1955–1959

    Diem skipped the 1956 elections and, with American money, built an anti-communist state. In 1959 Hanoi approved armed struggle in the South.

  2. 2

    What they expected

    Washington thought aid and police training would make Diem’s government strong. Hanoi thought guerrillas could bring Diem down without drawing in American troops.

    What they got wrong.Diem’s land policy and his favoritism toward Catholics turned villagers against him. Each side also misjudged how far the other would go.

  3. 3

    What happened

    Guerrillas killed more of Diem’s village officials, the countryside grew less safe, and the NLF formed in December 1960.

    The response.John F. Kennedy took office in 1961 and sent many more military advisers, Special Forces and helicopter units.

  4. 4

    Where it led

    An argument over who should rule Vietnam became a shooting war in the villages.

What each side wantedWashington and Hanoi, judged by the same questions

Washington

Who decided: Dwight D. Eisenhower, John Foster Dulles, Edward Lansdale, Ngo Dinh Diem

What they wanted

Build a stable, independent, non-communist South Vietnam with money and advisers, and keep American troops out of combat.

What they assumed

  • Aid and training from the Military Assistance Advisory Group could make South Vietnam able to defend itself.

  • A divided Vietnam was better than a united one under Ho Chi Minh, which Washington counted as a Cold War loss.

  • Ngo Dinh Diem was the only nationalist who opposed both the communists and the French and could take charge in Saigon.

What they argued about

  • In 1955 Ambassador J. Lawton Collins warned that Diem’s favoritism toward his family and his narrow support would bring trouble. He urged Washington to find someone else.

  • CIA officer Edward Lansdale and Secretary of State Dulles persuaded Eisenhower to stick with Diem.

Pressures at home

  • Eisenhower’s New Look policy kept military spending down and relied on nuclear weapons and alliances. It avoided sending large ground armies to Asia.

Hanoi

Who decided: Le Duan, Ho Chi Minh, Truong Chinh, Pham Van Dong

What they wanted

Rebuild the North after the war, strengthen socialism there and reunite the country.

What they assumed

  • At first Hanoi expected the Geneva Accords and diplomatic pressure to deliver nationwide elections in 1956.

  • The Soviet Union and China urged patience and political struggle, because they did not want to draw American troops in.

What they argued about

  • Truong Chinh wanted to put the North’s economy and land reform first. Le Duan, who had led the fight in the South, said that waiting while Diem hunted down southern communists would destroy them.

  • Le Duan’s side won in 1959 with Resolution 15, which approved armed struggle in the South.

Pressures at home

  • The land reform campaign of 1953 to 1956 went too far in many villages. Farmers rose up in Nghe An, and Ho Chi Minh publicly apologized.

Resolution 15 of the Vietnam Workers’ Party Central Committee

Central Executive Committee, Workers’ Party of Vietnam, Ruling Politburo / Central Committee, Hanoi, January 1959

Marked the decisive transition from political agitation to armed revolutionary warfare in the South, creating Group 559 to construct what became the Ho Chi Minh Trail.

A summary in our words, not a quotation

Adopted in January 1959, Resolution 15 designated violent struggle as the essential path for the revolution in South Vietnam, authorizing southern cadre to combine popular political mobilization with armed operations to dismantle the southern administration.

A temporary truce line becomes a Cold War border

The 1954 Geneva Declaration called the line at the 17th parallel a temporary military line. It was not supposed to be a political border.

But neither the United States nor the State of Vietnam signed the declaration. As French troops left and Washington backed Ngo Dinh Diem, the ceasefire line hardened into a fortified Cold War border.
