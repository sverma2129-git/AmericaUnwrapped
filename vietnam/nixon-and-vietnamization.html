Chapter 8, 1969–1972

Nixon and Vietnamization

Did Nixon believe South Vietnam could fight on its own?

Bringing the troops home took four years. During that time the war spread to Cambodia, and protest at home turned deadly at Kent State.

What happened

1969

Bringing the troops home

When Richard Nixon took office in 1969, Americans wanted the casualties to stop. Under the Nixon Doctrine and a policy called Vietnamization, he began bringing American ground troops home. Their number fell from a peak of 543,000 in 1969 to 24,000 by late 1972. Historians still argue about what Nixon was really after. Revisionist scholars say General Creighton Abrams’ One War plan to secure the countryside was actually working. Critics say Nixon and Henry Kissinger mainly wanted a decent interval: enough time between the American exit and South Vietnam’s fall that the fall would not look like an American defeat.

1969–1970

Cambodia, Kent State and Congress

To hit communist supply bases across the border, Nixon started Operation Menu in March 1969. B-52s secretly carpet-bombed neutral Cambodia, and the raids were hidden from Congress with a second set of records. In April 1970, American and South Vietnamese troops openly invaded Cambodia. Protests broke out on campuses across the country. On May 4, Ohio National Guardsmen shot and killed four students at Kent State University, and ten days later students were shot and killed at Jackson State. Congress answered with the Cooper-Church Amendment, which banned U.S. ground troops and advisers from Cambodia and Laos. It was the first time Congress had limited where troops could go during a war against a president’s wishes.

1971–1972

Vietnamization is tested

In February 1971, Operation Lam Son 719 put Vietnamization to the test. Three South Vietnamese divisions crossed into Laos along Route 9 to cut the Ho Chi Minh Trail, with no American ground troops or advisers going along. North Vietnam struck back with masses of tanks and heavy artillery. The South Vietnamese pullback became a panicked retreat, with soldiers clinging to the skids of helicopters. Washington said the raid slowed supplies from the North for a year. But it showed how exposed South Vietnam’s army was without American troops beside it. In March 1972, Hanoi launched the Easter Offensive, a full-scale invasion with hundreds of Soviet-supplied tanks. South Vietnamese forces held An Loc and Kontum, and won back Quang Tri after losing it, only because of massive American bombing in Operation Linebacker I and the mining of Haiphong harbor. The South’s army could hold with American airpower. Without it, it could not.

The short version

When Richard Nixon took office in 1969, he and National Security Adviser Henry Kissinger were under heavy pressure to stop American casualties. Under Vietnamization and the Nixon Doctrine, the U.S. began pulling out ground troops. Their number fell from 543,000 to 24,000 by late 1972. The fighting, and modern weapons, were handed to South Vietnam’s army, the ARVN. To destroy communist bases along the border, Nixon secretly ordered B-52s to bomb neutral Cambodia in Operation Menu. False records hid it from Congress. In April 1970, Nixon announced that ground troops were entering Cambodia, and campuses across the country went on strike. On May 4, Ohio National Guardsmen shot and killed four students at Kent State University. Congress answered with the Cooper-Church Amendment, which barred U.S. troops from Cambodia and Laos. It was the first time Congress had restricted troop deployments during a war. In 1971, the ARVN’s retreat in Operation Lam Son 719 in Laos went badly. In 1972, Hanoi launched its Easter Offensive with tanks and regular divisions. South Vietnam held with massive U.S. air support, called Operation Linebacker I. It could not have held alone.

How one decision played out

Follow one decision from this chapter to a result nobody planned.

  1. 1

    The decision

    Richard Nixon; Hanoi’s Politburo, 1969–1972

    Nixon pulled out ground troops but bombed more heavily, including in Cambodia and Laos. In 1972 Hanoi invaded the South with tanks in the Easter Offensive.

  2. 2

    What they expected

    Washington believed South Vietnam’s army could hold with American air support. Hanoi believed that army would break under a full tank assault.

    What they got wrong.South Vietnam came to depend on American air power it could not replace. Hanoi’s invasion lost huge numbers of men to B-52 strikes.

  3. 3

    What happened

    American deaths and draft calls fell while South Vietnamese losses rose. Both sides saw that the South could hold only with American planes overhead.

    The response.In October 1972, Henry Kissinger and Le Duc Tho reached a secret deal in Paris.

  4. 4

    Where it led

    The secret bombing and the 1970 invasion destabilized Cambodia and helped the Khmer Rouge rise to power.

What each side wantedWashington and Hanoi, judged by the same questions

Washington

Who decided: Richard M. Nixon, Henry Kissinger, Melvin Laird, Gen. Creighton Abrams, President Nguyen Van Thieu

What they wanted

Bring American troops home, reach peace with honor and use new ties with China and the Soviet Union to protect South Vietnam.

What they assumed

  • Historians disagree about whether Nixon and Kissinger thought South Vietnam’s army could survive alone, or only wanted a decent interval before it fell.

  • Nixon’s 1972 visit to Beijing and his deals with Moscow would lead both to press Hanoi toward a compromise.

What they argued about

  • Defense Secretary Melvin Laird wanted fast troop withdrawals to calm the country and protect the army’s morale.

  • Nixon and Kissinger preferred heavy bombing to keep the upper hand in the secret talks in Paris.

Pressures at home

  • Morale fell among the U.S. troops still there. Soldiers abused drugs, refused orders to fight and clashed along racial lines, and some attacked their own officers, which was called fragging.

  • In June 1971 Daniel Ellsberg leaked the Pentagon Papers, which showed that the government had misled the public about the war for years.

Hanoi

Who decided: Le Duan, Le Duc Tho, General Vo Nguyen Giap, Pham Van Dong

What they wanted

Survive the bombing, test South Vietnam’s army in big battles and keep North Vietnamese troops in the South.

What they assumed

  • Once American ground troops left, opposition at home would stop Washington from coming back.

  • Over time, the military balance in the South would swing Hanoi’s way.

What they argued about

  • North Vietnamese leaders were furious that Soviet and Chinese leaders hosted Nixon in 1972 while American bombs were falling on the North.

  • They argued over whether to launch a big tank offensive, which became the Easter Offensive, or save their forces for a longer war.

Pressures at home

  • Mines in Haiphong Harbor and bombed roads and rails under Linebacker I made it hard to move supplies.

Eyewitness reporting, Published Eyewitness Account

"Hanoi—1968" (Eyewitness Dispatch from North Vietnam)

Mary McCarthy, Author and Eyewitness Observer, May 23, 1968

Provides a rare, contemporaneous Western eyewitness record of how aerial bombardment operated in practice: rather than coercing capitulation, it stimulated decentralized civil defense and collective endurance.

A summary in our words, not a quotation

In her May 1968 eyewitness report from Hanoi, author Mary McCarthy described street-level life under American aerial bombardment, reporting individual precast concrete cylinder bomb shelters spaced along sidewalks and widespread evacuation of children and the elderly to rural villages, observing that bombardment reinforced civilian social mobilization and collective discipline rather than causing urban collapse.

Held by: The New York Review of Books, Vol. 10, No. 10
