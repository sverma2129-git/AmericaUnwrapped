Part 3, Chapter 8, 1969–1972

Nixon and Vietnamization

Was Nixon’s “Vietnamization” a real plan for South Vietnam to stand alone, or a way to buy a “decent interval” before it fell?

The withdrawal took four years. Along the way the war spread into Cambodia, campus protests such as Kent State shook the country, and Congress put new limits on the president’s power.

What happened

When Richard Nixon took office in 1969, Americans wanted the casualties to stop. Under the Nixon Doctrine and a policy called Vietnamization, he began bringing American ground troops home. Their number fell from a peak of 543,000 in 1969 to 24,000 by late 1972. Historians still argue about what Nixon was really after. Revisionist scholars say General Creighton Abrams’ One War plan to secure the countryside was actually working. Critics say Nixon and Henry Kissinger mainly wanted a decent interval: enough time between the American exit and South Vietnam’s fall that the fall would not look like an American defeat.

To hit communist supply bases across the border, Nixon started Operation Menu in March 1969. B-52s secretly carpet-bombed neutral Cambodia, and the raids were hidden from Congress with a second set of records. In April 1970, American and South Vietnamese troops openly invaded Cambodia. Protests broke out on campuses across the country. On May 4, Ohio National Guardsmen shot and killed four students at Kent State University, and ten days later students were shot and killed at Jackson State. Congress answered with the Cooper-Church Amendment, which banned U.S. ground troops and advisers from Cambodia and Laos. It was the first time Congress had limited where troops could go during a war against a president’s wishes.

In February 1971, Operation Lam Son 719 put Vietnamization to the test. Three South Vietnamese divisions crossed into Laos along Route 9 to cut the Ho Chi Minh Trail, with no American ground troops or advisers going along. North Vietnam struck back with masses of tanks and heavy artillery. The South Vietnamese pullback became a panicked retreat, with soldiers clinging to the skids of helicopters. Washington said the raid slowed supplies from the North for a year. But it showed how exposed South Vietnam’s army was without American troops beside it. In March 1972, Hanoi launched the Easter Offensive, a full-scale invasion with hundreds of Soviet-supplied tanks. South Vietnamese forces held key cities like An Loc and Quang Tri only because of massive American bombing in Operation Linebacker I and the mining of Haiphong harbor. The South’s army could hold with American airpower. Without it, it could not.

The short version

When Richard Nixon took office in 1969, he and National Security Adviser Henry Kissinger were under heavy pressure to stop American casualties. Under “Vietnamization” and the Nixon Doctrine, the U.S. began pulling out ground troops. Their number fell from 543,000 to 24,000 by late 1972. The fighting, and modern weapons, were handed to South Vietnam’s army, the ARVN. To destroy communist bases along the border, Nixon secretly ordered B-52s to bomb neutral Cambodia in Operation Menu. False records hid it from Congress. In April 1970, Nixon announced that ground troops were entering Cambodia, and campuses across the country went on strike. On May 4, Ohio National Guardsmen shot and killed four students at Kent State University. Congress answered with the Cooper-Church Amendment, which barred U.S. troops from Cambodia and Laos. It was the first time Congress had restricted troop deployments during a war. In 1971, the ARVN’s retreat in Operation Lam Son 719 in Laos went badly. In 1972, Hanoi launched its Easter Offensive with tanks and regular divisions. South Vietnam held with massive U.S. air support, called Operation Linebacker I. It could not have held alone.

For the test

What to know

  1. Vietnamization. The plan was to cut American casualties and calm protests at home. South Vietnamese infantry replaced American soldiers, while U.S. air power and supplies stayed.
  2. Cambodia and Kent State. Taking the war into Cambodia set off huge protests at home. They led to the Kent State shootings and closed universities across the country.
  3. Congress pushes back. Congress moved to limit the president’s war powers. It passed the Cooper-Church Amendment and repealed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution.

Key terms

Vietnamization
President Nixon’s policy of training and equipping South Vietnam’s forces to take over the fighting while American ground troops were withdrawn step by step.
Kent State Shootings (May 4, 1970)
Ohio National Guardsmen shot unarmed students at Kent State University during protests against the move into Cambodia. Four students were killed and nine were wounded.
Operation Menu (1969–1970)
The secret B-52 bombing of North Vietnamese bases inside neutral Cambodia. Its phases were code-named Breakfast, Lunch, Snack, Dinner and Dessert.

What Washington believed and what Hanoi believed

1969–1972
Who decided in WashingtonWhite House, Pentagon, CIA

What Washington wanted

Actors: Richard M. Nixon, Henry Kissinger, Melvin Laird, Gen. Creighton Abrams, President Nguyen Van Thieu

The goal

Withdraw U.S. combat forces, achieve “peace with honor,” and secure a sustainable position for South Vietnam through triangular diplomacy with China and the USSR.

What they assumed
  • Contested: Whether Nixon and Kissinger believed the ARVN could survive independently, or sought a “decent interval” to insulate American credibility from an eventual Saigon collapse.
  • Triangular diplomacy (the 1972 opening to Beijing and détente with Moscow) would induce Hanoi’s superpower patrons to pressure North Vietnam toward compromise.
What they argued about
  • Defense Secretary Laird prioritized rapid troop withdrawals to ease domestic unrest and protect military morale.
  • Nixon and Kissinger favored aggressive bombing escalations to preserve diplomatic leverage at the secret Paris negotiating table.
Who decided in HanoiPolitburo, Party Central Committee

What Hanoi wanted

Actors: Le Duan, Le Duc Tho, General Vo Nguyen Giap, Pham Van Dong

The goal

Withstand American aerial strikes, test the conventional capacity of the ARVN, and ensure North Vietnamese troops remained stationed in the South.

What they assumed
  • Once American ground combat forces departed, domestic political opposition would prevent Washington from re-intervening.
  • The balance of military forces in South Vietnam would shift irreversibly in Hanoi’s favor over time.
What they argued about
  • Outrage over Soviet and Chinese leaders hosting Nixon in Moscow and Beijing in 1972 while American bombs struck North Vietnam.
  • Tactical debates over whether to mount large-scale conventional armored operations (Easter Offensive) or husband forces for a longer struggle.

As U.S. troops left, the bombing grew

Between 1968 and 1972, the United States withdrew over 500,000 ground troops to de-escalate domestic political opposition. However, combat intensity did not decline proportionally: ground combat was transferred to the South Vietnamese army, subsidized by massive air bombardment.

What happened in 1971

Operation Lam Son 719: ARVN forces invade Laos along Route 9 to sever the Ho Chi Minh Trail without accompanying U.S. ground advisors. Met by massed North Vietnamese armor and heavy artillery, the retreat degenerates into panic.

Historians disagree:

Washington claimed Lam Son 719 disrupted enemy supply lines for months; critics and Hanoi cited it as proof that ARVN could not stand alone against North Vietnamese regulars without direct U.S. air and logistical backing.
