Chapter 9, 1972–1973, a turning point

The Paris Peace Accords

Secret talks, the Christmas Bombing, and America’s exit

Why did Nixon order the Christmas Bombing of 1972, and why did the 1973 Paris Peace Accords fail to end the war?

The U.S. got its troops and prisoners of war out. But the deal left 150,000 North Vietnamese troops inside South Vietnam, and that decided how the war would end.

The short version

In October 1972, secret talks in Paris between Henry Kissinger and the North Vietnamese diplomat Le Duc Tho produced a breakthrough. Hanoi dropped its demand that South Vietnam’s president, Nguyen Van Thieu, be removed. In return, the U.S. agreed that 150,000 to 200,000 North Vietnamese troops could stay where they were inside South Vietnam. Weeks before the 1972 presidential election, Kissinger announced that peace was close. When Thieu saw the draft, he rejected it in anger. He said that leaving northern troops on southern soil meant surrender. Talks broke down. From December 18 to 29, 1972, Nixon ordered Operation Linebacker II, the Christmas Bombing. B-52s dropped more than 20,000 tons of bombs on Hanoi and Haiphong, the heaviest strikes of the war. When talks resumed, the final Paris Peace Accords, signed on January 27, 1973, were almost the same as the October draft. Nixon got Thieu to sign by secretly threatening to cut off all aid. In private letters, he also promised that the U.S. would hit back hard if Hanoi broke the ceasefire. The last American combat troops left by March 29, 1973, and 591 American POWs came home.

What to know for the test

  1. The big concession. The 1973 Accords let North Vietnamese troops stay inside South Vietnam, while every American combat soldier had to leave.
  2. Why the Christmas Bombing. Linebacker II won few new terms from Hanoi. Its main purpose was to reassure Thieu and show American force before the U.S. left.
  3. Promises Congress cancelled. Nixon secretly promised Thieu the U.S. would strike back if Hanoi attacked. The Case-Church Amendment and the War Powers Act made that promise impossible to keep.

Key terms

Paris Peace Accords (January 27, 1973)
The Agreement on Ending the War and Restoring Peace in Viet-Nam. The United States, North Vietnam, South Vietnam and the Viet Cong all signed it.
Operation Linebacker II ("Christmas Bombing")
An 11-day bombing campaign by U.S. B-52s and fighter-bombers against Hanoi and Haiphong, from December 18 to 29, 1972.
Case-Church Amendment (June 1973)
A law passed by Congress that banned all U.S. military action, including bombing, in Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia after August 15, 1973.

What happened

The full story. The small numbers open the source behind each claim.

Beginning in 1969, Henry Kissinger conducted secret negotiations in a villa outside Paris with Le Duc Tho, a member of Hanoi’s Politburo. For three years, the talks deadlocked on two non-negotiable demands: Washington insisted that all North Vietnamese Army (PAVN) troops withdraw completely from the South, while Hanoi insisted that South Vietnamese President Nguyen Van Thieu be removed before any political settlement.

In October 1972, the impasse broke through mutual concessions: Hanoi dropped its demand for Thieu’s immediate ouster, agreeing he could remain head of the Saigon administration. In exchange, the United States conceded that approximately 150,000 to 200,000 North Vietnamese combat troops could remain in place inside South Vietnam. Weeks before the 1972 U.S. presidential election, Kissinger announced publicly: "Peace is at hand."

When presented with the draft, President Thieu vehemently rejected it, arguing that permitting foreign troops on sovereign South Vietnamese soil was a death warrant. Kissinger returned to Paris with 69 amendments demanded by Thieu, causing talks to break down. From December 18 to 29, 1972, Nixon unleashed Operation Linebacker II—the "Christmas Bombing"—dropping over 20,000 tons of explosives on Hanoi and Haiphong. Historians debate the bombing’s purpose: while administration officials claimed it forced Hanoi back to the table, the final Paris Peace Accords signed on January 27, 1973 were essentially identical to the October draft. The primary effect was demonstrating violent resolve to coerce Thieu into signing under the cover of Nixon’s secret written promises of air retaliation if Hanoi violated the ceasefire. All remaining U.S. combat troops departed by March 29, 1973, and 591 American POWs returned home.

Key dates

  1. Oct 8, 1972

    A breakthrough in Paris

    Hanoi drops its demand that Thieu go. The U.S. lets North Vietnamese troops stay in the South.

  2. Oct 26, 1972

    Kissinger says peace is near

    Days before the U.S. election, Kissinger tells reporters that peace is close.

  3. Dec 13, 1972

    The talks break down

    Hanoi rejects 69 changes demanded by Thieu and Washington, and the talks stop.

  4. Dec 18–29, 1972

    The Christmas Bombing

    Eleven days of all-out B-52 raids on Hanoi and Haiphong. Missiles shoot down 16 B-52s.

  5. Jan 5, 1973

    Nixon’s secret letter to Thieu

    Nixon promises to strike back quickly and hard if Hanoi breaks the peace agreement.

  6. Jan 27, 1973

    The Paris Peace Accords are signed

    All four sides sign. The U.S. agrees to pull out completely within 60 days.

Cause and effect

One decision from this chapter, followed step by step to a result nobody planned.

  1. 1

    The decision

    Kissinger, Nixon, & Le Duc Tho, January 27, 1973

    Signed the Paris Peace Accords, withdrawing all U.S. forces while leaving ~150,000 North Vietnamese troops inside South Vietnam.

  2. 2

    What they missed

    They assumed.American airpower could serve as a credible external deterrent to enforce the treaty.

    The risk nobody checked.Nixon’s deterrence rested entirely on personal executive threats, which evaporated as Watergate dismantled his political standing.

  3. 3

    What happened next

    First.591 American POWs returned home; the last U.S. combat troops left Vietnam on March 29, 1973.

    The other side’s answer.Neither North nor South Vietnam ceased firing; a violent "war of the flags" for contested territory erupted immediately.

  4. 4

    What it led to

    The Accords ended direct American participation without ending the civil war, leaving South Vietnam structurally vulnerable.

The question this leaves: How would Congress dismantle the legal authority for American military re-intervention, setting the stage for the final offensive?

What Washington believed and what Hanoi believed

1972–1973
Who decided in WashingtonWhite House, Pentagon, CIA

What Washington wanted

Actors: Richard M. Nixon, Henry Kissinger, President Nguyen Van Thieu, Alexander Haig

The goal

Retrieve American POWs, withdraw the remaining U.S. military forces, and preserve a defensible political settlement for South Vietnam.

What they assumed
  • Contested: Whether Nixon believed his threats of renewed B-52 strikes would deter a future invasion, or recognized that American re-intervention was politically impossible.
  • Congress would continue to approve robust military and financial aid pipelines to sustain the ARVN.
What they argued about
  • Thieu accused Kissinger of sacrificing South Vietnam’s sovereignty by permitting enemy combat units to remain in the South.
  • Nixon warned Thieu that if Saigon refused to sign, the United States would conclude a bilateral agreement with Hanoi and terminate all aid immediately.
Who decided in HanoiPolitburo, Party Central Committee

What Hanoi wanted

Actors: Le Duc Tho, Le Duan, Nguyen Duy Trinh, Pham Van Dong

The goal

Secure the complete and irreversible withdrawal of American military forces while keeping PAVN troops positioned inside South Vietnam.

What they assumed
  • Once U.S. forces departed and American domestic opposition solidified, the U.S. would be politically incapable of re-entering the war.
  • The balance of military forces in South Vietnam would irreversibly tilt in Hanoi’s favor over time.
What they argued about
  • Hanoi endured Linebacker II, calculating that shooting down B-52s and exhausting U.S. domestic tolerance would preserve their core diplomatic gains.
