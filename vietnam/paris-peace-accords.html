The Paris Peace Accords

Why didn’t the Paris Peace Accords end the war?

The United States got its troops and prisoners of war out. About 150,000 North Vietnamese troops stayed inside South Vietnam, and they decided how the war would end.

What happened

1969–1972

Secret talks outside Paris

Starting in 1969, Henry Kissinger held secret talks in a house outside Paris with Le Duc Tho, a member of Hanoi’s ruling Politburo. For three years, the talks were stuck on two demands neither side would drop. Washington insisted that every North Vietnamese soldier leave the South. Hanoi insisted that South Vietnam’s president, Nguyen Van Thieu, be removed before any political deal.

October 1972

A draft deal

In October 1972, both sides gave ground. Hanoi dropped its demand that Thieu go right away and agreed that he could stay in charge in Saigon. In return, the United States agreed that about 150,000 to 200,000 North Vietnamese combat troops could stay where they were inside South Vietnam. A few weeks before the 1972 presidential election, Kissinger told reporters that peace was at hand.

December 1972 – March 1973

The Christmas Bombing and the final deal

When Thieu saw the draft, he rejected it outright. Letting North Vietnamese troops stay on South Vietnamese soil, he said, was a death sentence for his country. Kissinger went back to Paris with 69 changes Thieu wanted, and the talks broke down. From December 18 to 29, 1972, Nixon ordered Operation Linebacker II, known as the Christmas Bombing. More than 20,000 tons of bombs fell on Hanoi and Haiphong. Historians argue about why. The administration said the bombing forced Hanoi back to the table, but the Paris Peace Accords signed on January 27, 1973, were almost the same as the October draft. Its main effect was on Thieu. The bombing, together with Nixon’s secret written promises to bomb again if Hanoi broke the ceasefire, pushed him into signing. The last U.S. combat troops left by March 29, 1973, and 591 American prisoners of war came home.

The short version

In October 1972, secret talks in Paris between Henry Kissinger and the North Vietnamese diplomat Le Duc Tho produced a breakthrough. Hanoi dropped its demand that South Vietnam’s president, Nguyen Van Thieu, be removed. In return, the U.S. agreed that 150,000 to 200,000 North Vietnamese troops could stay where they were inside South Vietnam. Weeks before the 1972 presidential election, Kissinger announced that peace was close. When Thieu saw the draft, he rejected it in anger. He said that leaving northern troops on southern soil meant surrender. Talks broke down. From December 18 to 29, 1972, Nixon ordered Operation Linebacker II, the Christmas Bombing. B-52s dropped more than 20,000 tons of bombs on Hanoi and Haiphong, the heaviest strikes of the war. When talks resumed, the final Paris Peace Accords, signed on January 27, 1973, were almost the same as the October draft. Nixon got Thieu to sign by secretly threatening to cut off all aid. In private letters, he also promised that the U.S. would hit back hard if Hanoi broke the ceasefire. The last American combat troops left by March 29, 1973, and 591 American POWs came home.

Key dates

  1. Oct 8, 1972

    A breakthrough in Paris

    Hanoi drops its demand that Thieu go. The U.S. lets North Vietnamese troops stay in the South.

  2. Oct 26, 1972

    Kissinger says peace is near

    Days before the U.S. election, Kissinger tells reporters that peace is close.

  3. Dec 13, 1972

    The talks break down

    Hanoi rejects 69 changes demanded by Thieu and Washington, and the talks stop.

  4. Dec 18–29, 1972

    The Christmas Bombing

    Eleven days of all-out B-52 raids on Hanoi and Haiphong. Missiles shoot down 16 B-52s.

  5. Jan 5, 1973

    Nixon’s secret letter to Thieu

    Nixon promises to strike back quickly and hard if Hanoi breaks the peace agreement.

  6. Jan 27, 1973

    The Paris Peace Accords are signed

    All four sides sign. The U.S. agrees to pull out completely within 60 days.

    What they expected

    The threat of American air power would keep Hanoi from breaking the deal.

    591 American prisoners of war came home, and the last U.S. combat troops left on March 29, 1973.

What each side wantedWashington and Hanoi, judged by the same questions

Washington

Who decided: Richard M. Nixon, Henry Kissinger, President Nguyen Van Thieu, Alexander Haig

What they wanted

Get the American prisoners of war back, bring the last troops home and leave South Vietnam in a position it could defend.

What they assumed

  • Historians disagree about whether Nixon thought his threat of more B-52 strikes would stop a future invasion, or knew Congress would never allow it.
  • Congress would keep sending South Vietnam the military and economic aid it needed.

What they argued about

  • Thieu accused Kissinger of giving away South Vietnam’s independence by letting enemy troops stay in the South.
  • Nixon told Thieu that if he refused to sign, the United States would make its own deal with Hanoi and cut off all aid.

Hanoi

Who decided: Le Duc Tho, Le Duan, Nguyen Duy Trinh, Pham Van Dong

What they wanted

Get every American soldier out for good while keeping North Vietnamese troops inside South Vietnam.

What they assumed

  • Once American forces left and opinion at home hardened, Washington would be unable to return to the war.
  • Over time, the military balance in the South would swing Hanoi’s way.

What they argued about

  • Hanoi rode out Linebacker II. Its leaders bet that shooting down B-52s and waiting out American patience would protect what they had won at the table.

International diplomatic record, International Treaty

Agreement on Ending the War and Restoring Peace in Viet-Nam (Paris Accords)

William P. Rogers, Nguyen Duy Trinh, Tran Van Lam, Nguyen Thi Binh, Signatories representing USA, DRV (North Vietnam), RVN (South Vietnam), PRG (Viet Cong), January 27, 1973

President Thieu viewed the treaty as a fatal compromise for South Vietnam, acquiescing only after Nixon secretly promised in personal letters that the U.S. would "respond with full force" if Hanoi violated the agreement—a commitment Congress soon rendered legally impossible via the Case-Church Amendment.

The exact words

“The United States and all other countries respect the independence, sovereignty, unity, and territorial integrity of Viet-Nam as recognized by the 1954 Geneva Agreements on Viet-Nam.”

Held by: United Nations Treaty Series, Vol. 935, No. 13295
