Part 1, Chapter 3, 1961–1963

Kennedy, Diem and the coup

How did Kennedy’s plan to fight guerrillas fail at Ap Bac, and why did Washington back the coup that killed Ngo Dinh Diem?

The 1963 coup broke South Vietnam’s government. After it, the United States was far more responsible for keeping Saigon standing.

What happened

John F. Kennedy took office in 1961 in the middle of crises in Laos, Berlin and Cuba’s Bay of Pigs. He wanted to fight what Soviet leaders called wars of national liberation without sending large army units into combat, as in Korea. His answer was flexible response, with a focus on fighting guerrillas. He sent Green Beret Special Forces and helicopter units, and he approved tests of chemicals that kill plants. By late 1963, the number of U.S. military personnel in South Vietnam had grown from about 900 to more than 16,300. That buildup happened well before Diem was killed.

In 1962, South Vietnam and its American advisers started the Strategic Hamlet Program. They built more than 8,000 fortified villages to cut farmers off from the Viet Cong. Many villagers hated it. They were forced off land where their families had farmed and were buried, and corrupt local officials made things worse. In January 1963, the Battle of Ap Bac showed how weak the South Vietnamese army still was. It outnumbered the enemy eight to one and had armored vehicles and air support. A Viet Cong battalion of 350 men beat it anyway.

In May 1963, government troops fired on Buddhist protesters in Hue, and the Buddhist Crisis began. On June 11, the monk Thich Quang Duc burned himself to death on a Saigon street in protest. Malcolm Browne’s photograph of it shocked people around the world. In August, Diem’s brother Ngo Dinh Nhu, who ran the security forces, raided Buddhist pagodas across the country. Washington answered with State Department Cable 243, drafted by Roger Hilsman. It signaled to South Vietnamese generals that the United States would back a new government if Diem did not get rid of Nhu. On November 1–2, 1963, the generals overthrew Diem and killed him and Nhu. The United States knew the coup was coming and did not stop it. Three weeks later, Kennedy was assassinated in Dallas.

The short version

President John F. Kennedy did not want to send large numbers of regular troops. He tried to beat the Viet Cong with “Flexible Response”: Green Berets, helicopters, chemical defoliants such as Agent Orange, and the Strategic Hamlet Program, which moved farmers into fortified villages. In January 1963, at the Battle of Ap Bac, 350 Viet Cong guerrillas beat 1,500 South Vietnamese (ARVN) troops who had American armored vehicles and helicopters. That spring, Diem’s government banned Buddhist flags and crushed protests. The monk Thich Quang Duc burned himself to death in Saigon in protest. The Kennedy administration decided Diem could not lead and sent Cable 243, which signaled support for a coup. On November 1–2, 1963, ARVN generals overthrew and killed Diem and his brother Nhu. A series of unstable military governments followed.

For the test

What to know

  1. More advisers. Under Kennedy, American military “advisers” grew from about 900 to more than 16,000, and many took part in combat.
  2. The Buddhist Crisis. The monk Thich Quang Duc burned himself to death in public. The outrage around the world cost Diem’s government its standing abroad.
  3. After the coup. Diem was killed on November 2, 1963, three weeks before Kennedy. South Vietnam lost its one steady leader, and the U.S. was tied more closely to its defense.

Key terms

Flexible Response
Kennedy’s defense policy. It replaced Eisenhower’s nuclear “Massive Retaliation” with more options: counterinsurgency, Special Forces (Green Berets), and regular forces matched to the size of the threat.
Strategic Hamlet Program
A joint U.S. and South Vietnamese program that forced farmers to move into fortified villages. Barbed wire and sharpened bamboo stakes were meant to cut them off from the Viet Cong.
State Department Cable 243 (August 24, 1963)
A top-secret telegram drafted by Roger Hilsman and approved by Kennedy. It told Ambassador Henry Cabot Lodge Jr. that the U.S. would no longer accept Diem’s brother Nhu in power, and would support a coup if Diem refused to remove him.

What Washington believed and what Hanoi believed

1961–1963
Who decided in WashingtonWhite House, Pentagon, CIA

What Washington wanted

Actors: John F. Kennedy, Robert S. McNamara, Henry Cabot Lodge Jr., Roger Hilsman, McGeorge Bundy

The goal

Defeat communist insurgency via counterinsurgency techniques while pressuring Diem into administrative and military reforms.

What they assumed
  • Modern American military techniques (helicopters, defoliants, special forces) could solve the political challenge of rural guerrilla warfare.
  • A military government led by ARVN generals would enjoy broader popular support and prosecute the war more effectively than Diem.
What they argued about
  • The anti-Diem faction (Harriman, Hilsman, Ambassador Lodge) argued that Diem was hopelessly alienated from the population and made victory impossible.
  • The pro-Diem faction (Vice President Johnson, Gen. Maxwell Taylor, CIA Director John McCone) warned that ousting Diem would produce chronic political chaos.
Who decided in HanoiPolitburo, Party Central Committee

What Hanoi wanted

Actors: Le Duan, Nguyen Chi Thanh, Ho Chi Minh, Vo Nguyen Giap

The goal

Dismantle the Strategic Hamlet network, defeat ARVN forces in the field, and exploit the political instability in Saigon.

What they assumed
  • The Saigon regime was an unstable coalition held together primarily by American backing; Diem’s fall would paralyze the southern administration.
  • The United States would hesitate to commit regular conventional ground combat forces if the insurgency maintained high operational tempo.
What they argued about
  • In December 1963, at the 9th Party Plenum, Le Duan pushed through Resolution 9, committing regular North Vietnamese Army (PAVN) regiments to infiltrate south and deliver a decisive blow before the U.S. could intervene directly.
  • Moderate party figures who urged caution regarding direct confrontation with American forces were sidelined.
