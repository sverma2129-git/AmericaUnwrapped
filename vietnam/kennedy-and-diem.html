Part 1, Chapter 31961–1963

Kennedy, Diem and the coup

Why did the United States help remove the leader it had backed for nine years?

After the coup, one general after another tried to run South Vietnam. Having helped push Diem out, the United States now had to hold Saigon together itself.

What happened

1961–1963

Kennedy sends advisers instead of armies

John F. Kennedy took office in 1961 in the middle of crises in Laos, Berlin and Cuba’s Bay of Pigs. He wanted to fight what Soviet leaders called wars of national liberation without sending large army units into combat, as in Korea. His answer was flexible response, with a focus on fighting guerrillas. He sent Green Beret Special Forces and helicopter units, and he approved tests of chemicals that kill plants. By late 1963, the number of U.S. military personnel in South Vietnam had grown from about 900 to more than 16,300. That buildup happened well before Diem was killed.

1962–1963

Strategic hamlets and the defeat at Ap Bac

In 1962, South Vietnam and its American advisers started the Strategic Hamlet Program. They built more than 8,000 fortified villages to cut farmers off from the Viet Cong. Many villagers hated it. They were forced off land where their families had farmed and were buried, and corrupt local officials made things worse. In January 1963, the Battle of Ap Bac showed how weak the South Vietnamese army still was. It outnumbered the enemy eight to one and had armored vehicles and air support. A Viet Cong battalion of 350 men beat it anyway.

May–November 1963

The Buddhist crisis and the coup

In May 1963, government troops fired on Buddhist protesters in Hue, and the Buddhist Crisis began. On June 11, the monk Thich Quang Duc burned himself to death on a Saigon street in protest. Malcolm Browne’s photograph of it shocked people around the world. In August, Diem’s brother Ngo Dinh Nhu, who ran the security forces, raided Buddhist pagodas across the country. Washington answered with State Department Cable 243, drafted by Roger Hilsman. It signaled to South Vietnamese generals that the United States would back a new government if Diem did not get rid of Nhu. On November 1–2, 1963, the generals overthrew Diem and killed him and Nhu. The United States knew the coup was coming and did not stop it. Three weeks later, Kennedy was assassinated in Dallas.

The short version

President John F. Kennedy did not want to send large numbers of regular troops. He tried to beat the Viet Cong with Flexible Response: Green Berets, helicopters, chemical defoliants such as Agent Orange, and the Strategic Hamlet Program, which moved farmers into fortified villages. In January 1963, at the Battle of Ap Bac, 350 Viet Cong guerrillas beat 1,500 South Vietnamese (ARVN) troops who had American armored vehicles and helicopters. That spring, Diem’s government banned Buddhist flags and crushed protests. The monk Thich Quang Duc burned himself to death in Saigon in protest. The Kennedy administration decided Diem could not lead and sent Cable 243, which signaled support for a coup. On November 1–2, 1963, ARVN generals overthrew and killed Diem and his brother Nhu. A series of unstable military governments followed.

    The Kennedy administration and South Vietnamese generals, August–November 1963

    Washington signaled in Cable 243 that it would accept a coup. Generals overthrew Diem and killed him and his brother Nhu.

    What they expected

    A new military government would pull the country together and fight harder.

    What they got wrong.Diem was the one leader holding Saigon’s quarreling generals together. Without him, they turned on each other.

    What happened

    Saigon went through a string of short-lived military governments. Desertions rose, and the Viet Cong took over more of the countryside.

    By helping remove Diem, the United States made itself responsible for whatever came next in Saigon.

What each side wantedWashington and Hanoi, judged by the same questions

Washington

Who decided: John F. Kennedy, Robert S. McNamara, Henry Cabot Lodge Jr., Roger Hilsman, McGeorge Bundy

What they wanted

Beat the guerrillas with counterinsurgency while pushing Diem to reform his government and army.

What they assumed

  • Helicopters, chemical defoliants and Special Forces could solve what was really a political fight for the villages.
  • A government led by South Vietnamese generals would win more support and fight better than Diem.

What they argued about

  • Averell Harriman, Roger Hilsman and Ambassador Henry Cabot Lodge argued that Diem had lost his people and could not win.
  • Vice President Johnson, General Maxwell Taylor and CIA Director John McCone warned that removing Diem would bring chaos.

Hanoi

Who decided: Le Duan, Nguyen Chi Thanh, Ho Chi Minh, Vo Nguyen Giap

What they wanted

Break up the strategic hamlets, beat South Vietnam’s army in the field and take advantage of the turmoil in Saigon.

What they assumed

  • Saigon’s government was held together mostly by American support, and Diem’s fall would leave it paralyzed.
  • The United States would hesitate to send combat troops as long as the guerrillas kept up the pressure.

What they argued about

  • In December 1963 Le Duan won approval for Resolution 9, which sent regular North Vietnamese army regiments south to strike before the United States could step in.
  • Party leaders who urged caution about fighting American forces lost influence.

U.S. government record, TOP SECRET // OPERATIONAL IMMEDIATE

State Department Cable 243 to Embassy Saigon (The Coup Cable)

George Ball, Averell Harriman, and Roger Hilsman, U.S. Department of State, August 24, 1963

The ensuing November 1, 1963 coup and assassination of Diem eliminated the civilian leadership of South Vietnam, creating a cycle of military juntas that drew Washington into direct institutional ownership of the regime.

The exact words

“US Government cannot tolerate situation in which power lies in Nhu's hands. Diem must be given chance to rid himself of Nhu and his coterie and replace them with best military and political personalities available. If, in spite of all of your efforts, Diem remains obdurate and refuses, then we must face the possibility that Diem himself cannot be preserved.”

Held by: Foreign Relations of the United States (FRUS), 1961–1963, Vol. IV
