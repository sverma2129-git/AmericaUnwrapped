Chapter 7, 1968

The Tet Offensive

Why did a battle Hanoi lost turn Americans against the war?

For two years Johnson’s officials had said victory was close. After Tet, many Americans stopped believing them, and Johnson began to pull back.

What happened

Late 1967 – January 1968

Khe Sanh, and then the cities

In late 1967, North Vietnamese forces surrounded the Marine base at Khe Sanh, near the DMZ. Washington feared it could become an American Dien Bien Phu, and Westmoreland’s attention stayed fixed on it. Khe Sanh was partly a diversion. In the early hours of January 30–31, 1968, during a holiday truce for Tet, the lunar new year, communist forces attacked 36 of 44 provincial capitals, five major cities and dozens of military bases across South Vietnam.

January–February 1968

The embassy and Hue

In Saigon, a 19-man Viet Cong commando unit blew a hole in the wall around the U.S. Embassy. All of the attackers were killed or captured within hours, but live television showed dead Americans on the embassy grounds. In Hue, two North Vietnamese army regiments seized the old walled Citadel. U.S. Marines and South Vietnamese troops spent 26 days retaking it room by room, and more than 80 percent of the city was destroyed. While they held Hue, communist officials executed between 2,800 and 3,000 people, including government workers, teachers, Catholics and foreigners.

February–March 1968

Losing the argument at home

As a battle, Tet was a failure for Hanoi. The mass uprising its leaders expected, the Tong Khoi Nghia, never happened. The Viet Cong lost more than 40,000 experienced fighters, and from then on, soldiers drafted in the North did most of the fighting. But in the United States, Tet shook the public’s belief that the war was being won. On February 27, 1968, CBS anchor Walter Cronkite told viewers the war was “mired in stalemate.” When the military asked for 206,000 more troops, Johnson’s senior civilian advisers, known as the Wise Men, told him to scale back. On March 31, 1968, Johnson announced a partial halt to the bombing and said he would not run for reelection.

The short version

In late 1967, General Westmoreland told Americans the war was being won and that the end was in sight. Early on January 30–31, 1968, during the truce for Tet, the lunar new year, more than 84,000 North Vietnamese and Viet Cong troops attacked at the same time. They hit 36 of 44 provincial capitals, five major cities and dozens of bases across South Vietnam. In Saigon, a 19-man squad blew a hole in the wall of the U.S. Embassy, and morning television showed dead Americans on the grounds. In Hue, communist forces took the old Citadel and held it for 26 days. They executed nearly 3,000 civilians before U.S. Marines retook the city. As a battle, Tet was a defeat for Hanoi. The uprising it expected never came, and the Viet Cong lost more than 40,000 experienced fighters. As politics, it destroyed the administration’s credibility. CBS anchor Walter Cronkite told viewers the war was “mired in stalemate,” and Johnson is reported to have said that losing Cronkite meant losing Middle America. On March 31, 1968, Johnson announced a partial bombing halt and withdrew from the presidential race.

Key dates

  1. Jan 21, 1968

    The siege of Khe Sanh begins

    Two North Vietnamese divisions surround 6,000 Marines for a 77-day artillery battle.

  2. Jan 30–31, 1968

    The Tet Offensive begins

    Attacks hit cities across the country at once. Commandos break into the U.S. Embassy grounds in Saigon.

  3. Jan 31 – March 2

    The Battle of Hue

    26 days of street fighting retake Hue. Mass graves of executed civilians are found.

  4. Feb 27, 1968

    Walter Cronkite’s broadcast

    Cronkite gives his own view that the war is stuck in a stalemate, and many Americans agree.

  5. March 16, 1968

    The My Lai massacre

    U.S. soldiers murder more than 500 unarmed civilians in Son My village. It stays secret until late 1969.

  6. March 31, 1968

    Johnson drops out

    Johnson stops bombing north of the 20th parallel and quits the presidential race.

    Le Duan in Hanoi; Lyndon Johnson, January–March 1968

    Hanoi attacked the cities during the Tet holiday truce. Afterward, Johnson stopped sending more troops and opened peace talks.

    What they expected

    Hanoi expected the people of the cities to rise up. Washington expected its battlefield win to restore Americans’ faith in the war.

    The Viet Cong lost more than 40,000 fighters. Johnson’s presidency fell apart, and American escalation stopped.

    From then on, the war’s outcome depended less on battles in Vietnam than on politics in the United States.

What each side wantedWashington and Hanoi, judged by the same questions

Washington

Who decided: Lyndon B. Johnson, Clark Clifford, Gen. William Westmoreland, Walter Cronkite, Sen. Eugene McCarthy

What they wanted

Make the cities safe again, defeat the offensive and calm voters shocked by the violence.

What they assumed

  • The enemy had suffered huge losses, so Americans should see Tet as a win.

  • News coverage had turned a battlefield win into a defeat in people’s minds.

What they argued about

  • Westmoreland and General Earle Wheeler asked for 206,000 more troops to strike while the enemy was weak, including bases in Laos and Cambodia.

  • Defense Secretary Clark Clifford called together the Wise Men, senior statesmen such as Dean Acheson. They told Johnson that Americans would not support what victory would take.

Pressures at home

  • The anti-war movement surged into mainstream electoral politics; Eugene McCarthy and Robert Kennedy challenged LBJ in primary elections.

  • Assassination of Martin Luther King Jr. in April 1968 ignited urban riots across more than 100 American cities.

Hanoi

Who decided: Le Duan, General Vo Nguyen Giap, General Van Tien Dung, Tran Van Tra

What they wanted

Break Saigon’s government with a general offensive and uprising, and force the United States out.

What they assumed

  • People in South Vietnam’s cities hated Thieu’s government and would rise up once the attacks began.

  • South Vietnamese army units would mutiny, fall apart or switch sides.

What they argued about

  • Le Duan overruled Giap, who had warned that attacking the cities would expose their forces.

  • Afterward, field commanders were blamed for the unrealistic plan, the heavy losses and the uprising that never came.

Pressures at home

  • Tet destroyed much of the Viet Cong in the South. For the rest of the war, Hanoi filled southern units almost entirely with draftees from the North.

A summary in our words, not a quotation

The December 1967 military directive formulated operational goals for the Tet Offensive, calling for synchronized assaults against southern urban administrative centers and military headquarters under the doctrinal assumption that urban populations would stage a general uprising that would dismantle the Saigon military and force American withdrawal.

Saigon and the U.S. Embassy

Fighting inside the American embassy grounds, on morning television.

At 2:47 a.m. on January 31, 19 Viet Cong commandos blew a hole in the embassy’s outer wall with explosives. Other squads hit the Presidential Palace, the national radio station and Tan Son Nhut Air Base. Within six hours every commando at the embassy had been killed or captured.

What it cost Hanoi
The attackers took none of the key government buildings, the city did not rise up, and Hanoi lost some of its best-trained fighters.
What it did to Washington
Television showed wounded Americans and dead attackers on the embassy grounds. Westmoreland had just said victory was in sight.

Why Tet is a puzzle

On the battlefield, Tet was a disaster for the communist side. About 40,000 Viet Cong fighters were killed, and the uprising Hanoi expected never came. At home in the United States, the attacks on dozens of cities at once wrecked the story that the war was being won. Within two months, Walter Cronkite had called the war a stalemate, Westmoreland was on his way out of Vietnam, and Johnson had decided not to run again.
