Chapter 11, 1975–1995

After the war

What did thirty years of war cost Vietnam?

About 3 million Vietnamese died, and more than a million later fled by sea. Washington and Hanoi took twenty years after the fall of Saigon to restore normal relations.

What happened

1975–1986

A planned economy fails

After the victory, Hanoi formally reunited the country in July 1976 as the Socialist Republic of Vietnam. The new government ran the economy from the top. It took over private businesses in the South and forced farmers in the Mekong Delta onto collective farms. With a U.S. trade embargo, the loss of Western aid and poor management on top of that, farm output collapsed. By the late 1970s there was rationing and near-famine, and in 1986 inflation rose above 700 percent.

1975–1980s

Camps and forced moves

The new government also locked up its former enemies. Hundreds of thousands of former South Vietnamese military officers, government workers and religious leaders were sent to re-education camps, trai cai tao in Vietnamese. They had been told to expect a few political lectures. Instead, officers and senior officials were held for 3 to more than 10 years of forced labor and political lessons. At the same time, the New Economic Zones program forced about 750,000 to more than 1 million people from southern cities onto undeveloped land in the jungle, while about 1 million northerners moved south.

1975–1995

The boat people and new wars

Between 1975 and 1995, somewhere between 800,000 and nearly 2 million refugees fled Indochina by sea. The crisis peaked in 1978–1979, as relations with China broke down. Vietnam’s government turned on its ethnic Chinese citizens, the Hoa. It seized their property and drove hundreds of thousands of them into boats on the South China Sea, and in some months the Hoa made up 50 to 80 percent of new arrivals at refugee camps. International agencies estimate that 200,000 to 500,000 people died at sea from drowning, exposure and pirate attacks. The region did not find peace either. In December 1978, Vietnam invaded Cambodia and overthrew the Khmer Rouge, which had committed genocide there. In February 1979, China invaded northern Vietnam to punish it. With its economy failing, Vietnam’s Communist Party adopted Doi Moi, or Renovation, at its 6th Congress in December 1986. It ended collective farming and moved the country toward a market economy.

The short version

The 1954–1975 war cost a huge number of lives. 58,220 American service members were killed. Between 200,000 and 250,000 South Vietnamese (ARVN) soldiers died. Official Vietnamese figures put North Vietnamese and Viet Cong deaths at 1.1 million, and about 2 million civilians died on both sides. After the fall of Saigon, Hanoi forced a strict socialist system on the South. About one million former southern officers, officials and intellectuals were held in harsh re-education camps. Up to 165,000 died from hunger, disease and forced labor. From 1975 to the early 1990s, more than 1.5 million Vietnamese escaped in small wooden boats. These were the boat people, and an estimated 200,000 to 400,000 died at sea from drowning and pirate attacks. In the 1990s, Vietnam opened its economy with market reforms called Doi Moi. Two veteran senators, John McCain and John Kerry, worked across party lines to find out what happened to soldiers missing in action (MIA). In July 1995, President Bill Clinton restored normal diplomatic relations with Vietnam.

Key dates

  1. 1975–1985

    Re-education camps and collective farms

    More than 1 million people go through the camps. Southern farms are forced into collectives, and food runs short.

  2. July 2, 1976

    Vietnam is officially reunited

    North and South become one country. Saigon is renamed Ho Chi Minh City.

  3. Nov 1978

    The Hai Hong

    Malaysia turns away a ship carrying 2,500 refugees. The UN refugee agency starts treating boat people as refugees as a group.

  4. Dec 1978 – Jan 1979

    Vietnam invades Cambodia

    Vietnamese forces overthrow Pol Pot’s Khmer Rouge and then fight guerrillas there for ten years.

  5. Feb–March 1979

    China and Vietnam go to war

    China invades northern Vietnam to punish it. Both sides lose many soldiers.

  6. Dec 1986

    Doi Moi reforms begin

    The 6th Party Congress drops central planning and collective farms and adopts market reforms.

What each side wantedWashington and Hanoi, judged by the same questions

Washington

Who decided: Gerald Ford, Jimmy Carter, Ronald Reagan, United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees

What they wanted

Take in refugees, keep the trade embargo on Vietnam, account for Americans missing in action and help Vietnam’s neighbors push it out of Cambodia.

What they assumed

  • Cutting Vietnam off from trade and diplomacy would force Hanoi out of Cambodia and make it act more cautiously.

What they argued about

  • Talks about restoring relations stalled in the late 1970s, after Vietnam invaded Cambodia and gave the Soviet navy use of Cam Ranh Bay.

Pressures at home

  • The Vietnam syndrome: many Americans and members of Congress did not want to send troops overseas again.

  • Hundreds of thousands of Vietnamese, Hmong and Cambodian refugees settled in American towns and cities.

Hanoi

Who decided: Le Duan, Nguyen Van Linh, Truong Chinh, Vo Van Kiet

What they wanted

Bring socialism to the South, crush opponents, defend the borders with Cambodia and China and fix a failing economy.

What they assumed

  • Leaders thought the North’s planned economy could be imposed on the South’s market economy by decree.

  • Historians disagree about what pushed Vietnam toward Doi Moi: the Western embargo, or collapse at home, runaway inflation and shrinking Soviet aid.

What they argued about

  • Hardliners feared that markets would water down the party’s ideas. Southern reformers such as Vo Van Kiet and Nguyen Van Linh showed that private markets and family farms were needed to keep people fed.

Pressures at home

  • Prices rose about 700% in 1986, some rural provinces came close to famine, and soldiers kept dying in Cambodia and along the Chinese border.

Associated Press Wire Service, Journalistic Wire Archive, April 4, 1995

Documents the primary disclosure of North Vietnamese military fatalities while illustrating how even official civilian casualty tallies have been reported with competing ranges (2 million combined vs. 4 million total).

A summary in our words, not a quotation

On April 4, 1995, an Associated Press wire reported the Vietnamese government’s release of comprehensive casualty statistics for the 1954–1975 war, disclosing that 1.1 million North Vietnamese and Viet Cong combatants were killed and 600,000 wounded, alongside civilian casualty estimates of approximately 2 million dead across Vietnam.

Where the numbers come from

On April 4, 1995, shortly before relations with Washington were restored, Hanoi published its war dead for the first time: 1.1 million soldiers killed, 600,000 wounded and about 2 million civilians killed. A 1995 study by Hirschman, Preston and Vu put total Vietnamese deaths at about 3.1 million.
